### Background: Understanding Statistics in Everyday Life

Statistical information influences decisions in education, transportation, public health, agriculture, business, and community development. A school principal may investigate learners' preferred extracurricular activities before establishing clubs. A barangay council may collect information about household waste disposal to improve environmental programs. A school canteen manager may examine daily sales records before deciding which food products to prepare. In each situation, the decision becomes more informed when relevant information is systematically collected, organized, and interpreted.

**Statistics** is a branch of mathematics concerned with collecting, organizing, presenting, analyzing, and interpreting data. Rather than relying entirely on personal opinions, statistics provides methods for examining observable patterns. For example, the statement that most students prefer volleyball is a claim that requires evidence. A properly conducted survey can help determine how many students actually prefer volleyball and how that number compares with the preferences for other sports.

A statistical investigation begins with a clearly defined question. Investigators must identify the information needed, determine the individuals or objects to be studied, choose an appropriate data-collection method, and establish how observations will be recorded. If these decisions are poorly designed, even mathematically accurate calculations may produce conclusions that do not represent the intended population.

After data collection, observations must be organized. A list containing thirty responses can be difficult to interpret because repeated values appear in different positions. A frequency distribution table arranges those observations into categories and reports how often each occurs. This structure makes comparisons easier and reveals which values or categories appear most frequently.

Statistical graphs provide another level of interpretation. Bar graphs compare category frequencies; line graphs reveal changes across an ordered sequence, commonly time; pie graphs show how categories contribute to a complete whole; and stem-and-leaf plots organize numerical values while preserving the individual observations. Selecting a suitable representation is therefore as important as constructing it correctly.

Statistics does not automatically establish the cause of an observed pattern. Suppose attendance increases during one month. A graph can demonstrate the increase, but it cannot independently establish whether the change resulted from improved transportation, fewer illnesses, school activities, or other factors. Sound statistical interpretation requires careful distinction between what the data show and what additional evidence would be needed to explain the pattern.

Throughout this lesson, learners will develop the mathematical foundations needed to design simple investigations, use statistical representations accurately, and explain numerical conclusions.

## SECTION 2. POPULATIONS, SAMPLES, AND STATISTICAL VARIABLES

### 2.1 Meaning and Purpose of Statistical Investigation

A statistical investigation is a structured process of answering questions by examining collected data. Its foundation is the identification of the population being studied and the characteristic to be measured. Without a clear population, the investigator cannot determine which observations are relevant or whether a selected group reasonably represents the larger community.

A **population** is the complete set of individuals, objects, or observations that an investigation intends to study. For example, if a school investigates the transportation methods used by all enrolled Grade 7 students, the population consists of every Grade 7 learner enrolled in that school. The population is defined by the investigation, not necessarily by the size of a geographic area.

A **sample** is a subset of the population selected for observation. When a population is large, collecting information from every member may require substantial time and resources. A sample makes an investigation more manageable, provided that the selection process is suitable for the research question. For example, a sample of 80 learners drawn from a population of 400 may provide useful information about transportation patterns.

A **census** collects information from every member of the population. A census and a sample survey may investigate the same characteristic, but they differ in coverage. A census provides complete population coverage when successfully conducted, whereas a sample survey studies only selected members. A sample survey may be more practical, but its conclusions remain subject to sampling uncertainty and possible selection bias.

A **variable** is a characteristic that can take different values among the individuals or objects studied. A student's preferred sport, travel time to school, number of siblings, and daily water consumption are examples of variables. The variable determines what information will be recorded and what statistical representation will be appropriate.

Statistical variables may be categorical or numerical. A categorical variable identifies a quality or classification, such as preferred transport or favorite school subject. A numerical variable represents a number obtained by counting or measuring. The number of books borrowed in a month is a discrete numerical variable because it takes countable whole-number values. The time required to travel to school is a continuous numerical variable because it can be measured with increasingly fine precision.

### 2.3 Distinguishing Categorical and Numerical Data

Consider the following observations about six learners:

| Learner | Transport Method | Travel Time (minutes) | Number of Siblings |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Ana | Walking | 12 | 2 |
| Ben | Jeepney | 25 | 1 |
| Carlo | Walking | 15 | 3 |
| Dana | Bicycle | 18 | 2 |
| Ella | Jeepney | 30 | 0 |
| Farah | Walking | 10 | 4 |

The transport method is categorical because its values are names of categories. Travel time is continuous numerical data because it is measured, even though the recorded measurements are rounded to whole minutes. The number of siblings is discrete because siblings are counted as whole individuals.

This distinction determines how the variables should be analyzed. It would be inappropriate to compute an average of category names such as walking and jeepney. However, travel times can be arranged numerically, compared, and summarized using measurements.

### 2.4 Mathematical Reasoning: Why Representativeness Matters

Suppose a school has 400 Grade 7 learners, with 200 attending morning classes and 200 attending afternoon classes. A researcher wants to estimate the proportion who walk to school but surveys only 40 learners from morning classes.

Although the sample contains valid learners from the school, its selection excludes the afternoon group. If transportation arrangements differ between schedules, the resulting sample may not accurately reflect the entire Grade 7 population.

The difficulty is not arithmetic. The difficulty is the sampling design. A carefully collected small sample may provide better evidence than a larger sample selected through a systematically biased procedure.

## SECTION 3. DATA COLLECTION AND SAMPLING TECHNIQUES

### 3.1 Methods of Collecting Data

Different investigations require different data-collection techniques. The selected method must correspond to the information needed and the characteristics of the population. A researcher investigating learners' reading preferences may use a questionnaire, whereas one studying pedestrian traffic near a school gate may use direct observation.

A **survey** collects responses from individuals, usually through a structured questionnaire or interview. Questions should be clear, neutral, and suitable for the respondents. A question such as "Which mode of transportation do you usually use to reach school?" identifies the intended information without encouraging a particular response.

An **interview** collects information by asking respondents questions directly. Interviews allow clarification when a response is unclear and may provide detailed explanations. However, the interviewer must avoid leading questions that encourage predetermined answers. Consistency is especially important when responses from different individuals will be compared.

**Observation** involves recording events or characteristics as they occur. Counting vehicles passing a crossing during a specified interval is an observational method. Observational records should use consistent procedures. Counting at different times of day without acknowledging the difference may produce misleading comparisons.

**Experiments** involve deliberately changing or controlling conditions to examine their effects. For example, a science class may compare the germination of seeds placed under different watering conditions. Experimental data are statistical observations, but investigating cause-and-effect relationships requires attention to fair testing and variables that could influence the outcome.

**Existing records** can also provide data. Attendance sheets, library borrowing logs, and official rainfall records contain information collected previously. Before using existing records, the investigator must determine whether their dates, units, definitions, and completeness fit the current investigation.

### 3.2 Sampling Techniques

Sampling techniques describe how members of the population are selected. Sampling decisions affect the reliability and limitations of the final conclusions.

In **simple random sampling**, every possible sample of a given size has an equal chance of selection. One practical method is to assign a unique number to every member of the population and randomly select the required numbers without replacement. A random sample reduces systematic selection bias when the procedure is properly implemented, although chance variation remains.

In **systematic sampling**, researchers choose observations at a fixed interval from an ordered list after selecting a random starting position. For example, from a list of 200 learners, selecting every tenth name after a randomly chosen starting point among the first ten positions produces a sample of 20. This method is convenient, but the ordering of the list must not contain patterns that distort the selection.

In **stratified sampling**, the population is divided into meaningful groups called strata. Random samples are then selected from the strata. For example, learners may be grouped by grade section, and a proportionate number may be randomly selected from each section. This technique helps ensure that relevant subgroups are represented.

In **convenience sampling**, the investigator selects readily available respondents. Interviewing the first twenty learners encountered near a school entrance is convenient, but the respondents may not represent the wider population. Convenience sampling can be useful for preliminary investigations, yet conclusions about the entire population require particular caution.

### 3.4 Worked Example: Determining a Systematic Sampling Interval

**Problem:** A school has 240 Grade 7 learners. A researcher plans to select 30 learners using systematic sampling. Determine the sampling interval.

**Given:** Population size, N = 240 learners; desired sample size, n = 30 learners.

**Find:** Sampling interval, k.

> **FORMULA CARD - SYSTEMATIC SAMPLING INTERVAL**
>
> **k = N ÷ n**
>
> Where:
>
> - k = number of list positions between successive selected observations
> - N = total number of population members
> - n = desired number of sampled members
>
> This simple form applies when N is divisible by n and members are arranged in an appropriate list.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the population: N = 240 learners.
2. Identify the sample size: n = 30 learners.
3. Substitute the given values: k = 240 ÷ 30.
4. Perform division: k = 8.
5. Verify the selection count: 240 ÷ 8 = 30 learners.

**Final Answer: The sampling interval is 8.**

Select a random starting position from 1 to 8, then select every eighth position. If the random starting position is 5, the selected positions begin 5, 13, 21, 29, and continue at equal intervals until 30 positions have been selected.

The random starting position matters because it prevents the researcher from always favoring individuals who occupy the same fixed initial position in the list.

### 3.5 Common Misconceptions

**Misconception 1: A large sample is automatically representative.**

A large sample selected from an unrepresentative subgroup can produce substantial bias.

**Misconception 2: Random sampling means choosing any available person without planning.**

Random selection requires a process based on chance, not arbitrary personal preference.

**Misconception 3: Statistical data must always consist of numbers.**

Categorical information is also statistical data. Names of transport methods, preferred activities, and product categories can be counted and compared.

**Misconception 4: Every survey response is necessarily accurate.**

Respondents may misunderstand questions, provide incomplete responses, or report information inaccurately. Clear instructions and careful data checking remain necessary.

## SECTION 4. FREQUENCY DISTRIBUTION TABLES AND STATISTICAL REPRESENTATION

### 4.1 Organizing Raw Data

Raw data are the original observations collected during an investigation. Before making a statistical conclusion, an investigator should inspect the data for missing responses, inconsistent categories, and possible recording errors.

Consider a survey asking twenty learners about their preferred mode of transportation to school. The recorded responses are:

Walking, Jeepney, Walking, Bicycle, Jeepney, Walking, Bus, Bicycle, Walking, Jeepney, Bus, Walking, Bicycle, Jeepney, Walking, Bus, Jeepney, Bicycle, Walking, Jeepney.

The responses can be organized by counting the number of occurrences of each category.

| Transportation Method | Tally | Frequency |
|---|---|---:|
| Walking | Five and two | 7 |
| Jeepney | Five and one | 6 |
| Bicycle | Four | 4 |
| Bus | Three | 3 |
| **TOTAL** | | **20** |

A **frequency** is the number of times a particular value or category appears in a data set. A **frequency distribution table** organizes those frequencies systematically, allowing learners to compare categories without recounting the original observations.

The total frequency must equal the number of valid observations included in the data set. Here, 7 + 6 + 4 + 3 = 20, which agrees with the twenty survey responses.

### 4.2 Relative Frequency and Percentage Frequency

Relative frequency describes the proportion of observations belonging to a particular category. Dividing the category frequency by the total number of observations produces a value between 0 and 1, inclusive.

> **FORMULA CARD - FREQUENCY AND PERCENTAGE**
>
> **Relative Frequency = f ÷ N**
>
> **Percentage Frequency = (f ÷ N) × 100%**
>
> Where:
>
> - f = frequency of a selected category, measured in observations
> - N = total number of valid observations, measured in observations
>
> Relative frequency has no unit. Percentage frequency is expressed as a percentage of the complete data set.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1A - Calculating Percentage Frequency

**Problem:** Seven out of twenty learners walk to school. What percentage of the surveyed learners walk?

**Given:** f = 7 learners; N = 20 learners.

**Find:** Percentage of learners who walk.

**Formula:** Percentage = (f ÷ N) × 100%.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Write the formula: Percentage = (f ÷ N) × 100%.
2. Substitute: Percentage = (7 ÷ 20) × 100%.
3. Divide: 7 ÷ 20 = 0.35.
4. Convert to percentage: 0.35 × 100% = 35%.
5. Verify: 35% of 20 learners is 7 learners.

**Final Answer: 35% of the surveyed learners walk to school.**

The complete frequency distribution is:

| Transportation Method | Frequency | Relative Frequency | Percentage |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Walking | 7 | 0.35 | 35% |
| Jeepney | 6 | 0.30 | 30% |
| Bicycle | 4 | 0.20 | 20% |
| Bus | 3 | 0.15 | 15% |
| **TOTAL** | **20** | **1.00** | **100%** |

The percentage total verifies that the categories collectively account for the entire set of twenty recorded observations.

### 4.3 Selecting a Suitable Statistical Graph

An appropriate statistical representation depends on the nature of the data and the purpose of the investigation.

A **bar graph** is used to compare categories or separate discrete values. Categories are commonly placed along the horizontal axis, while their frequencies appear on the vertical axis. The bars should have equal widths and a common scale, with spaces separating categories.

A **pie graph** divides a circle into sectors representing parts of a complete whole. Each sector corresponds to the proportion or percentage represented by a category. The sum of all sector angles must equal 360°, and the sum of all percentages must equal 100%.

A **line graph** represents how numerical values change over an ordered sequence, such as dates, months, or consecutive observation periods. Points are plotted and connected in sequence. It is particularly useful for examining increases, decreases, and fluctuations over time.

A **stem-and-leaf plot** arranges numerical observations according to their place values. In a two-digit data set, the tens digit may serve as the stem and the ones digit as the leaf. Unlike a frequency table that reports only category totals, a stem-and-leaf plot preserves the original numerical values.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1B - Constructing a Pie Graph

**Problem:** Construct a pie graph representing the twenty learners' transportation preferences.

**Given:** Walking = 7; Jeepney = 6; Bicycle = 4; Bus = 3; total = 20.

**Find:** Sector angle for each transportation category.

> **FORMULA CARD - SECTOR ANGLE**
>
> **Sector Angle = (f ÷ N) × 360°**
>
> Where:
>
> - f = frequency of a selected category
> - N = total frequency
> - 360° = angle of a complete circle

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the total: N = 20.
2. Calculate walking: (7 ÷ 20) × 360° = 126°.
3. Calculate jeepney: (6 ÷ 20) × 360° = 108°.
4. Calculate bicycle and bus: (4 ÷ 20) × 360° = 72°; (3 ÷ 20) × 360° = 54°.
5. Verify the complete circle: 126° + 108° + 72° + 54° = 360°.

**Final Answer: Walking = 126°; Jeepney = 108°; Bicycle = 72°; Bus = 54°.**

| Category | Frequency | Percentage | Sector Angle |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Walking | 7 | 35% | 126° |
| Jeepney | 6 | 30% | 108° |
| Bicycle | 4 | 20% | 72° |
| Bus | 3 | 15% | 54° |
| **TOTAL** | **20** | **100%** | **360°** |

The resulting pie graph represents the survey data correctly because every sector angle is proportional to its corresponding category frequency.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.1C - Constructing a Stem-and-Leaf Plot

**Problem:** Twelve learners record the following quiz scores:

18, 22, 25, 19, 31, 27, 24, 18, 33, 29, 21, 26.

**Given:** Twelve two-digit quiz scores.

**Find:** An ordered stem-and-leaf plot.

**Principle:** The tens digits are stems; the ones digits are leaves.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Arrange the scores in ascending order: 18, 18, 19, 21, 22, 24, 25, 26, 27, 29, 31, 33.
2. Identify the distinct tens digits: 1, 2, and 3.
3. Place the ones digits beside the corresponding stem.
4. Arrange the leaves in ascending order within each row.
5. Count the leaves to confirm that all twelve observations appear.

| Stem | Leaves |
|---|---|
| 1 | 8 8 9 |
| 2 | 1 2 4 5 6 7 9 |
| 3 | 1 3 |

**Key: 2 | 5 represents a score of 25.**

**Final Answer: The stem-and-leaf plot contains all twelve scores, arranged according to their tens and ones digits.**

The plot shows that seven learners obtained scores in the twenties. The minimum score is 18, and the maximum score is 33.

### 4.4 Interpreting Statistical Representations

A statistical graph should be interpreted in relation to its title, labels, measurement units, scale, and source data. Accurate interpretation involves identifying what a graph measures before comparing its numerical values.

When interpreting a bar graph, the heights or lengths of the bars must be compared using the numerical scale. The tallest bar represents the greatest displayed frequency. However, if the graph's scale does not begin at zero, differences between bar heights may appear more dramatic than the underlying numerical differences.

A pie graph communicates proportions rather than changes over time. A sector occupying one-quarter of the circle represents 25% of the complete data set. Without knowing the total number of observations, a sector percentage alone does not reveal how many individuals are represented.

A line graph communicates trends over ordered observations. An upward segment represents an increase between consecutive plotted values. A downward segment represents a decrease. A single increase does not automatically demonstrate a long-term upward trend, and a downward trend does not by itself establish the cause of a change.

> **MATHEMATICAL WATCH OUT:** A graph can be mathematically accurate while still being misleading if it omits essential context, uses an unsuitable scale, or presents a selected group as though it represents an entire population.

## SECTION 5. MATHEMATICAL REASONING AND ERROR ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Verifying a Frequency Distribution

An important mathematical principle is that each valid observation must be counted exactly once when the categories are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive.

Suppose the data consist of the recorded transportation preference of twenty learners, but the frequency table reports Walking = 7, Jeepney = 6, Bicycle = 4, and Bus = 4.

The frequencies add to 21 rather than 20. This difference reveals an inconsistency that must be investigated before the data can be interpreted.

The error may have occurred because a response was counted twice, a tally was copied incorrectly, or a category frequency was recorded inaccurately. Regardless of its source, the discrepancy prevents the table from accurately representing the original observations.

A useful verification rule is:

> **FORMULA CARD - TOTAL FREQUENCY**
>
> **N = f₁ + f₂ + f₃ + f₄**
>
> Where:
>
> - N = number of valid observations
> - f₁, f₂, f₃, f₄ = frequencies of four distinct, nonoverlapping categories
>
> For a different number of categories, include one frequency term for each category.

For the transportation example:

N = 7 + 6 + 4 + 3 = 20.

The total agrees with the number of observations.

### 5.2 Detecting Misleading Scales

A bar graph represents two school sections with 40 and 44 learners. If the vertical axis begins at zero, the difference between the bars is small relative to their total heights. If the axis begins at 39, the second bar may appear five times as tall as the first visible bar segment.

The numerical difference remains four learners. The visual impression changes because of the truncated scale.

This example demonstrates why readers should inspect axis labels and scales rather than relying only on visual appearance. Proper statistical reasoning evaluates the recorded numerical values and the method used to display them.

### 5.3 Distinguishing Observation from Explanation

Consider a hypothetical line graph of library visitors showing 28 visitors on Monday and 40 on Tuesday.

The data support the statement that twelve more visitors were recorded on Tuesday than on Monday.

They do not establish why the increase occurred. A statement that the library attracted more visitors because of a new reading program would require additional evidence identifying the program and its possible effects.

**Observation:** Visitor count increased by 12.

**Possible explanation:** An activity, schedule change, or another factor may have influenced attendance.

**Evidence-based conclusion:** Tuesday recorded more visitors than Monday in the observed data.

### 5.4 Common Error Analysis

| Incorrect Reasoning | Mathematical Problem | Correct Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| A larger sample cannot be biased | Sample size does not eliminate systematic selection errors | Examine both sample size and selection method |
| A 40% pie sector measures 40° | Percentages and angles are different units | 40% × 360° = 144° |
| A frequency table can omit a valid observation without affecting its total | The total frequency would no longer match the complete data set | Count every valid observation in the defined data set |
| A line graph proves that a change was caused by one particular event | A trend alone does not establish causation | Additional evidence is necessary |
| The highest bar always represents the largest population category | A sampled graph describes only its observed data unless generalization is justified | Identify the population, sample, and context |
| A stem-and-leaf plot should ignore repeated values | Repeated observations are part of the data | Show one leaf for every occurrence |

### 5.5 Mathematical Reasoning Exercise

Two learners conduct separate surveys about school transportation.

Learner A randomly selects 60 students from the complete Grade 7 roster.

Learner B interviews 100 students waiting at a jeepney terminal.

Both report that jeepney is the most common transportation method among their respondents.

The two investigations do not necessarily provide equally reliable information about the entire Grade 7 population. Learner B's selection procedure favors students who use or pass through the terminal, creating a possible sampling bias. Learner A's probability-based selection procedure is better suited to estimating preferences across the defined Grade 7 population, assuming the roster and implementation are appropriate.

The central principle is that the quality of statistical evidence depends on how the data were obtained as well as how many observations were recorded.

## SECTION 6. APPLIED MASTERY - CONSTRUCTING AND INTERPRETING STATISTICAL GRAPHS

### 6.1 Choosing the Appropriate Graph

Selecting an appropriate statistical graph requires examining the type of data and the comparison being made. The same numerical data may sometimes be represented in different ways, but certain graphs communicate particular relationships more clearly.

A bar graph is suitable when comparing distinct categories. For example, a school canteen may record the number of sandwiches, bananas, rice meals, and bottled drinks sold in one day. A bar graph allows readers to compare these categories and identify which item sold most frequently.

A pie graph is useful when emphasizing how categories contribute to one complete set. If every learner selects exactly one preferred snack, the relative frequencies can be represented as sectors of a circle. The entire circle represents 100% of the responses. All category frequencies must refer to the same total.

A line graph is most appropriate when the horizontal axis represents an ordered progression, particularly time. Daily attendance, monthly rainfall, and weekly library visits can be represented using line graphs. The sequence of points helps reveal changes from one observation period to another.

A stem-and-leaf plot is useful for a manageable set of numerical observations when retaining individual values is important. Unlike a bar graph that groups responses into category frequencies, a stem-and-leaf plot displays the original observations in a compact arrangement.

The selection of a graph should therefore be based on the statistical question. Asking which snack is most popular requires a category comparison, while asking how sales changed from Monday to Friday requires a time-based comparison.

A Grade 7 class records the number of visitors to its reading corner over five school days.

**Problem:** Determine the overall change in visitor count from Monday to Friday, and identify the interval with the greatest increase.

**Given:** Monday = 12; Tuesday = 15; Wednesday = 13; Thursday = 18; Friday = 20.

**Find:** Overall increase and greatest increase between consecutive days.

> **FORMULA CARD - CHANGE BETWEEN OBSERVATIONS**
>
> **Change = Final Value - Initial Value**
>
> Where:
>
> - Final Value = the value at the end of the comparison period
> - Initial Value = the value at the beginning of the comparison period
> - Change = increase or decrease, in the same unit as the measured variable
>
> A positive change indicates an increase; a negative change indicates a decrease.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the starting and ending counts: 12 visitors and 20 visitors.
2. Calculate the overall change: 20 - 12 = 8 visitors.
3. Calculate consecutive changes: Tuesday - Monday = 3; Wednesday - Tuesday = -2; Thursday - Wednesday = 5; Friday - Thursday = 2.
4. Compare the consecutive changes: 3, -2, 5, and 2.
5. Identify the largest increase: 5 visitors, from Wednesday to Thursday.

**Final Answer: The number of visitors increased by 8 from Monday to Friday. The greatest consecutive increase was 5 visitors, from Wednesday to Thursday.**

The data also indicate that Friday recorded the largest number of visitors. However, they do not explain why Friday had the highest count.

### Background: Extending the Number System

Numbers are indispensable tools for describing quantities, relationships, changes, and measurements. In earlier mathematics lessons, learners used counting numbers to represent quantities such as the number of books, people, objects, or activities. The whole numbers extended counting numbers by including zero, making it possible to describe the absence of a counted quantity. However, whole numbers alone are insufficient to represent every numerical situation encountered in daily life.

Consider a school cooperative that records money received and money owed. If the cooperative receives ₱500, the amount can be represented using a positive number. If it owes ₱200, the obligation may be represented by -200 in a signed balance model. The negative sign indicates a condition opposite to a positive balance. It does not mean that the cooperative possesses a physically negative quantity of money. Instead, it communicates a mathematical relationship involving debt.

A similar situation occurs when measuring elevation relative to sea level. A location 150 meters above sea level can be assigned the value +150 m, while a location 40 meters below sea level can be represented by -40 m. Sea level serves as the reference point, represented by zero. The positive and negative signs therefore provide directional information that an unsigned quantity cannot communicate independently.

Temperature scales also demonstrate the usefulness of negative numbers. A temperature of -4°C represents a value four degrees Celsius below the zero mark of the Celsius scale. A temperature of +4°C represents a value four degrees Celsius above the zero mark. Although the values have equal numerical distance from zero, the temperatures are not equal. Their opposite signs distinguish different positions on the temperature scale.

The **set of integers** includes the counting numbers, their negative counterparts, and zero. Integers are fundamental to algebra because they allow numerical operations and relationships to extend beyond nonnegative quantities. They also provide the mathematical basis for later studies involving signed numbers, coordinate systems, equations, numerical patterns, and measurements.

Integers can be represented concretely through counting chips and physically marked positions, pictorially through number lines and diagrams, and symbolically through numerals and mathematical inequalities. These different representations develop conceptual understanding by connecting familiar situations with abstract numerical relationships.

A number line is particularly valuable because it provides an ordered geometric representation of integers. Positive integers appear to the right of zero, while negative integers appear to the left. Each integer occupies a unique position, and distances between consecutive integers are equal when the number line is uniformly scaled.

By studying integers, learners move beyond viewing numbers only as quantities being counted. They begin to recognize that numbers can also describe direction, position, comparison, and distance. This understanding is essential for performing operations involving signed numbers in subsequent lessons.

## SECTION 2. UNDERSTANDING POSITIVE INTEGERS, NEGATIVE INTEGERS, AND ZERO

### 2.1 Defining the Set of Integers

An **integer** is a number belonging to a set containing zero, the positive counting numbers, and their negative counterparts. Integers do not include fractional or decimal values that are not whole-number values. Although 3.0 is written with a decimal point, it has the same numerical value as the integer 3. In contrast, 3.5 is not an integer because it lies between consecutive whole-number values.

The set of integers is commonly represented by the symbol **ℤ**. This notation allows mathematicians to refer to the complete collection without writing an extended sequence each time. The negative integers continue indefinitely in the negative direction, while positive integers continue indefinitely in the positive direction. The complete set is therefore infinite.

Integers can be classified into three groups: positive integers, negative integers, and zero. Positive integers are greater than zero and may be written with a plus sign, although the plus sign is usually omitted. Negative integers are less than zero and must be distinguished by the negative sign. Zero is neither positive nor negative.

The sign attached to an integer is important because it influences the number's position and mathematical interpretation. For example, 8 and -8 have the same numerical magnitude relative to zero but represent different values. In a balance model, a positive amount and a negative amount may describe credits and debts. In a position model, they may indicate opposite directions from the same reference point.

The distinction between signed values and their physical interpretation must be maintained. A bank balance of -₱250 does not represent a negative quantity of paper bills. Instead, it may describe an overdraft or an obligation of ₱250, depending on the situation. Likewise, -3°C is a measured temperature relative to the Celsius zero point, not the absence of temperature.

Understanding integers therefore requires recognizing both their symbolic structure and their practical meaning. Learners should be able to identify an integer, explain its sign, and determine an appropriate reference point before interpreting it in a particular context.

> **FORMULA CARD - THE INTEGER SET**
>
> **ℤ = {…, -4, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, …}**
>

>

**ℕ = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …}**

The whole numbers are:

**W = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, …}**

Every natural number is a whole number, and every whole number is an integer.

However, not every integer is a whole number because negative integers are not included in the set of whole numbers.

For example, -7 is an integer but is not a whole number. The number 0 is both a whole number and an integer, while 11 is a natural number, whole number, and integer.

### 2.4 Zero as a Reference Point

Zero plays a central role in signed-number systems because it separates the positive integers from the negative integers. The number line assigns zero a unique position, and every positive integer is located to its right while every negative integer is located to its left.

Zero must not be described as belonging to either the positive or negative group. A common misconception is that zero is positive because it appears immediately before the positive integers. Mathematical classification depends on the value, not merely the location of a number in a displayed sequence.

Zero can also represent a meaningful reference condition. In a temperature model, 0°C identifies a specific point on the Celsius scale. In a balance model, zero may indicate neither a credit balance nor a debt. In an elevation model, zero meters may represent sea level.

The interpretation of zero therefore depends on the reference system, but its classification as an integer does not change.

### 2.5 Identifying Opposite Integers

Two integers are **opposites**, or additive inverses, when they are the same distance from zero but lie on opposite sides of the number line.

Zero is its own opposite because changing the sign of zero does not change its value.

## SECTION 3. REPRESENTING INTEGERS IN REAL-LIFE SITUATIONS AND NUMBER LINES

### 3.1 Positive and Negative Numbers as Opposite Quantities

Integers provide a consistent mathematical method for representing opposite conditions. In practical situations, the signs attached to numbers often indicate direction relative to an identified reference point. The interpretation of the sign is not arbitrary once a convention has been established. It must remain consistent throughout the situation being analyzed.

For example, a school cooperative may record money received as positive and money owed as negative. An amount of +₱300 represents a positive balance or credit, while -₱300 represents an obligation of equal magnitude. The two values are opposites because they describe equal numerical amounts associated with contrary financial conditions.

Elevation provides another useful model. Sea level is represented by zero, heights above sea level by positive numbers, and depths below sea level by negative numbers. A mountain location at +650 m is above the reference level, while a submarine at -120 m is below that reference level. These values do not indicate the total distance between the two locations without additional calculation.

Movement along a straight line can also be represented using integers. If east is designated as positive, moving 9 meters east can be represented by +9 m, while moving 9 meters west can be represented by -9 m. If a different direction is chosen as positive, the numerical signs must be assigned consistently according to that convention.

Temperature can be represented using positive and negative values on scales that permit measurements below zero. The values +6°C and -6°C are distinct because they indicate points on opposite sides of zero. Their signs identify their positions relative to the Celsius reference point.

The meaning of an integer must always be established through context. Without a reference point or agreed sign convention, a signed number may be mathematically valid but insufficiently explained in a real-life problem. Correct mathematical communication therefore requires both the numerical value and the interpretation of the sign.

### 3.3 The Structure of a Number Line

A number line is a straight line on which numbers are represented by points at locations corresponding to their values. Integer number lines are constructed using equal unit intervals so that numerical differences correspond to geometric distances.

Zero is commonly placed near the center of a number line when both positive and negative numbers are displayed. Positive integers are placed in increasing order to the right, while negative integers appear in decreasing order to the left.

The equal-spacing condition is essential. If -4 and -3 are separated by one centimeter, then -3 and -2 must also be separated by one centimeter on the same uniform scale. Changing the spacing arbitrarily would distort the geometric meaning of numerical distance.

The number line extends indefinitely in both directions. Arrowheads at the ends indicate continuation beyond the displayed values. The point marked zero does not represent the beginning or end of all integers. It is the origin from which signed positions are measured.

Learners must interpret both direction and magnitude when locating integers. To locate -5, begin at zero and count five equal intervals to the left. To locate 5, count five equal intervals to the right. These positions are different even though their distances from zero are equal.

## SECTION 4. COMPARING AND ORDERING INTEGERS

### 4.1 The Order Relation

Integers can be compared using their positions on a number line. The fundamental ordering principle is that an integer farther to the right is greater than an integer farther to the left.

For example, 5 is greater than -2 because 5 is located to the right of -2. The comparison is expressed as **5 > -2**. Conversely, -2 is less than 5, written as **-2 < 5**.

Every positive integer is greater than zero, and zero is greater than every negative integer. Therefore, a negative integer is always less than a positive integer. This relationship is important because it provides an immediate method for comparing two integers with different signs.

When comparing two negative integers, learners must consider their positions rather than the numerical sizes of their unsigned parts. For example, -8 is less than -3 because -8 is farther to the left. The fact that 8 is greater than 3 does not mean that -8 is greater than -3. The negative sign changes the ordering relationship.

The symbols used in integer comparisons communicate precise mathematical statements. The symbol > means greater than, < means less than, and = means equal to. The symbols ≥ and ≤ mean greater than or equal to and less than or equal to, respectively.

### 4.2 Comparison Matrix

| First Integer | Second Integer | Correct Relationship | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 3 | 8 > 3 | 8 lies to the right of 3 |
| -4 | 2 | -4 < 2 | Every negative integer is less than every positive integer |
| -7 | -2 | -7 < -2 | -7 lies farther to the left |
| 0 | -9 | 0 > -9 | Zero is greater than every negative integer |
| -5 | -5 | -5 = -5 | The two integers are identical |
| -1 | -12 | -1 > -12 | -1 lies closer to zero and farther right |
| 6 | 0 | 6 > 0 | Every positive integer is greater than zero |

### 4.3 Ascending and Descending Order

Arranging integers in **ascending order** means writing the values from least to greatest. On a number line, the arrangement proceeds from left to right.

Arranging integers in **descending order** means writing them from greatest to least. On a number line, the arrangement proceeds from right to left.

The mathematical order does not depend on how many digits appear in the unsigned parts of negative integers. For example, -100 is less than -10 because it occupies a position farther to the left.

The correct ordering of integers requires attention to signs and positions. A useful procedure is to identify negative integers first, determine their relative order, locate zero if present, and then arrange positive integers.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.2A - ARRANGING INTEGERS

**Problem:** Arrange the integers 6, -11, 0, -4, 9, -8, and 2 in ascending order.

**Given:** 6, -11, 0, -4, 9, -8, 2.

**Find:** The ascending arrangement.

**Principle:** Smaller integers appear farther to the left on the number line.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the negative integers: -11, -4, and -8.
2. Arrange the negative integers from least to greatest: -11, -8, -4.
3. Locate zero after all negative integers.
4. Arrange the positive integers from least to greatest: 2, 6, 9.
5. Combine the groups and verify that each value is greater than the one immediately before it.

**Final Answer: -11, -8, -4, 0, 2, 6, 9.**

The result can also be written as:

**-11 < -8 < -4 < 0 < 2 < 6 < 9**

### 4.4 Comparing Values in Context

Suppose three underwater instruments are located at elevations of -25 m, -12 m, and -40 m relative to sea level. The instrument at -12 m is at the highest elevation because -12 is the greatest of the three signed values.

The instrument at -40 m is at the lowest elevation because -40 is the least integer. It is also the deepest below sea level among the three.

This example demonstrates why **highest elevation** and **greatest depth** must not be treated as identical ideas. The highest elevation is determined by comparing signed coordinates, while greatest depth below sea level is determined by comparing nonnegative distances below the reference level.

## SECTION 5. ABSOLUTE VALUE AND MATHEMATICAL REASONING

### 5.1 Meaning of Absolute Value

The **absolute value** of an integer is its distance from zero on the number line, measured in number-line units.

The absolute value of an integer is written using vertical bars. For example, |-5| represents the absolute value of -5.

Because distance cannot be negative, the absolute value of every integer is nonnegative. Negative integers therefore have positive absolute values, while positive integers have absolute values equal to themselves.

Zero has an absolute value of zero because it is located at the reference point.

> **FORMULA CARD - ABSOLUTE VALUE OF AN INTEGER**
>
> **|a| = a, when a ≥ 0**
>
> **|a| = -a, when a < 0**
>
> Where:
>
> - a = any integer
> - |a| = the distance of a from zero
> - -a = the opposite of a
>
> For a negative integer, taking its opposite produces a positive value.

### 5.2 Absolute Value Concept Matrix

| Integer | Absolute Value | Distance from Zero |
|---|---:|---|
| -12 | 12 | 12 units |
| -7 | 7 | 7 units |
| -1 | 1 | 1 unit |
| 0 | 0 | 0 units |
| 3 | 3 | 3 units |
| 9 | 9 | 9 units |
| 15 | 15 | 15 units |

### 5.3 Worked Example - Absolute Value and Opposite Integers

**Problem:** Determine |-9|, |9|, and |0|. Explain the relationship between the results.

**Given:** The integers -9, 9, and 0.

**Find:** The absolute value of each integer.

**Principle:** Absolute value measures distance from zero.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Locate -9 on the number line, nine units to the left of zero.
2. Count its distance from zero: |-9| = 9.
3. Locate 9 nine units to the right of zero: |9| = 9.
4. Locate zero at the origin: |0| = 0.
5. Compare the results: -9 and 9 have equal absolute values because both are nine units from zero.

**Final Answer: |-9| = 9, |9| = 9, and |0| = 0.**

### 5.4 Mathematical Reasoning: Opposites and Absolute Value

Opposite nonzero integers have equal absolute values. This property follows directly from the geometric structure of the number line.

For example, the points -6 and 6 are each six units from zero. Their signed positions differ, but their distances from zero are equal.

The principle can be expressed as:

**|a| = |-a|**

This relationship is valid for every integer a.

Absolute value should not be confused with making an entire expression positive without evaluating it. For example:

|-5| = 5, but -|-5| = -5.

In the second expression, the negative sign is outside the absolute-value bars. The absolute value is evaluated first, followed by the application of the external negative sign.

Similarly, |-4| and -4 are not equal. The first expression represents positive 4; the second represents negative 4.

### 5.5 Error Analysis: Common Misconceptions

| Incorrect Statement | Mathematical Error | Correct Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| The absolute value of -8 is -8 | Confuses signed position with distance | |-8| = 8 |
| -10 is greater than -3 because 10 is greater than 3 | Ignores integer ordering | -10 < -3 |
| Zero is a positive integer | Misclassifies zero | Zero is neither positive nor negative |
| The opposite of -7 is -7 | Does not reverse the sign | The opposite is 7 |
| Absolute value is always greater than zero | Excludes the zero case | |0| = 0 |
| -|-6| = 6 | Ignores the external negative sign | -|-6| = -6 |

### 5.6 Mathematical Investigation

Consider the integers -4 and 4.

Their absolute values are equal:

|-4| = 4 and |4| = 4.

However, -4 < 4.

The results demonstrate that **equal absolute value does not mean equal signed value**.

Now compare -9 and -2.

Since -9 lies farther left, -9 < -2.

However:

|-9| = 9 and |-2| = 2.

Therefore:

|-9| > |-2|.

This shows that the order of negative integers differs from the order of their absolute values. Absolute value compares distances, while signed comparison determines positions.

## SECTION 6. APPLIED MASTERY - MODELING INTEGERS IN REAL-WORLD SITUATIONS

### 6.1 Integers in Financial Records

Signed integers provide a simple way to represent credits, debts, and changes in financial records when amounts are whole pesos. A positive balance indicates an amount available in an account, while a negative balance may indicate an obligation or overdraft, depending on the financial system.

For example, a recorded balance of -₱450 represents an amount 450 pesos below a zero balance. Its absolute value is 450 pesos, which describes the magnitude of the negative balance without indicating that it is a credit.

This distinction becomes important when comparing two negative balances. A balance of -₱150 is greater than -₱600, although the second debt has the larger magnitude.

### 6.2 Integers in Elevation and Depth

Sea level provides a useful reference for signed elevation. A point 200 meters above sea level has elevation +200 m, while a point 75 meters below sea level has elevation -75 m.

If two underwater objects are located at -35 m and -80 m, the object at -35 m is higher. The object at -80 m is deeper because its distance below zero is greater.

Absolute value describes the magnitude of the elevation relative to sea level, but the signed coordinate communicates whether the location is above or below the reference level.

### 6.3 Integers in Temperature

The Celsius temperature scale includes positive and negative values. A temperature of -3°C is less than 0°C, while +8°C is greater than 0°C.

When comparing temperatures, the number-line order determines which temperature is warmer. For example, -2°C is warmer than -7°C because -2 is greater than -7.

The absolute value of a temperature tells how many Celsius degrees the reading is from zero on the scale, not how hot or cold it feels. A temperature of -10°C is colder than -2°C even though |-10| is greater than |-2|.

### Background: Addition Beyond Whole Numbers

Addition is one of the fundamental operations in mathematics. During earlier grade levels, learners investigated addition primarily through nonnegative quantities. They combined groups of objects, counted forward on a number line, and applied number properties to determine totals. The introduction of negative integers extends these familiar ideas and makes it possible to model changes in opposite directions.

Consider a school cooperative that records financial transactions. Receiving ₱500 may be represented by +500, while incurring an expense of ₱200 may be represented by -200. If both amounts are recorded as changes in the cooperative's cash position, the combined change is represented by (+500) + (-200). The sum is +300, indicating a net increase of ₱300.

The negative sign in this example does not mean that addition has changed into subtraction as an operation. Instead, it identifies the direction of the second quantity. Adding a negative integer means combining a signed quantity that points toward the negative direction. This distinction helps learners understand why the sum may be positive, negative, or zero.

Integer addition can be explained through three interconnected representations. The **concrete model** uses physical counters to represent positive and negative units. The **pictorial model** uses number lines, arrows, and drawings to illustrate signed movement. The **symbolic model** represents mathematical relationships using signed numerals, addition symbols, and equations.

Each representation provides a different form of mathematical evidence. Counters demonstrate how opposite quantities cancel, number lines show movement and final position, and symbolic expressions support efficient calculation. Moving among these representations helps learners understand numerical rules rather than memorizing procedures without meaning.

When integers have the same sign, their combined effect increases the magnitude in a common direction. Adding two positive integers produces a positive sum because both quantities contribute positively. Adding two negative integers produces a negative sum because both quantities contribute negatively.

When integers have different signs, the quantities partially or completely offset one another. Their combined value depends on the relative magnitudes of the positive and negative parts. If the magnitudes are equal, the sum is zero. If one magnitude is greater, the remaining amount determines both the sign and absolute value of the result.

Mathematical properties provide an additional basis for understanding integer addition. The commutative property shows that changing the order of addends does not change the sum. The associative property permits regrouping. The identity property explains the role of zero, while the additive inverse property establishes that opposite integers combine to produce zero.

The study of integer addition therefore provides a foundation for subtraction, multiplication, division, simplifying numerical expressions, and solving algebraic equations. Accurate integer addition also supports practical reasoning involving changes, balances, measurements, and comparisons.

## SECTION 2. ADDING INTEGERS USING COUNTERS AND ZERO PAIRS

### 2.1 Understanding Integer Counters

Integer counters are physical or pictorial objects used to represent signed numerical values. Two distinguishable types of counters are required: one type represents +1, and the other represents -1. Different colors may be used, provided that the meaning of each color is clearly established and applied consistently.

For this lesson, blue counters represent positive one, while orange counters represent negative one. Five blue counters therefore represent +5. Four orange counters represent -4. The numerical value of a collection depends on both the number and the type of counters it contains.

Counters support conceptual learning because each signed integer can be represented by a collection of units. For example, -6 corresponds to six negative counters. The representation makes the magnitude visible while preserving the sign that determines the quantity's direction or contribution.

When two sets of counters are combined, the resulting collection represents the sum of the corresponding integers. The learner must then determine whether any positive and negative counters can be paired and removed without changing the total value.

A positive counter has a value of +1, and a negative counter has a value of -1. Their combined value is zero because +1 + (-1) = 0. This combination is called a **zero pair**.

The zero-pair concept is essential to understanding addition with different signs. Removing a zero pair does not change the total value of a collection. Therefore, positive and negative counters may be matched and removed until only unmatched counters remain.

### 2.3 Adding Integers with the Same Sign

When two integers have the same sign, all counters represent contributions in the same direction. No zero pairs are produced by combining the counters because there are no counters of opposite signs to match.

For example, (+3) + (+4) is represented by three positive counters combined with four positive counters. The collection contains seven positive counters, so the sum is +7.

Similarly, (-3) + (-4) is represented by three negative counters combined with four negative counters. The resulting collection contains seven negative counters, so the sum is -7.

Both examples demonstrate a common principle: when the addends have the same sign, their absolute values are added, and the common sign is retained.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3A - ADDING NEGATIVE INTEGERS

**Problem:** Evaluate (-5) + (-3) using integer counters.

**Given:** First addend = -5; second addend = -3.

**Find:** The sum.

**Principle:** Combining counters with the same negative sign produces a negative total.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Represent -5 using five negative counters.
2. Represent -3 using three additional negative counters.
3. Combine the counters into one collection.
4. Count the total number of negative counters: 5 + 3 = 8.
5. Write the signed result: (-5) + (-3) = -8.

**Final Answer: -8.**

**Verification:** Both addends are negative, so their sum must be negative. The absolute values add to 8, confirming the result.

### 2.4 Adding Integers with Different Signs

When addends have different signs, positive and negative counters are combined in one collection. The learner pairs each positive counter with one negative counter. Each completed pair represents zero and can be removed without changing the value of the collection.

Consider (+7) + (-4). Seven positive counters and four negative counters are combined. Four positive counters form zero pairs with the four negative counters. Three positive counters remain, so the sum is +3.

In contrast, (-7) + (+4) produces seven negative counters and four positive counters. Four zero pairs are formed, leaving three negative counters. The sum is therefore -3.

The examples establish that the sign of a nonzero sum of integers with different signs depends on which addend has the greater absolute value.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3B - ADDING INTEGERS WITH DIFFERENT SIGNS

**Problem:** Evaluate (-9) + (+5) using zero pairs.

**Given:** First addend = -9; second addend = +5.

**Find:** The sum.

**Principle:** A positive counter and a negative counter form a zero pair.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Represent -9 using nine negative counters.
2. Represent +5 using five positive counters.
3. Match five positive counters with five negative counters.
4. Remove the five zero pairs. Four negative counters remain.
5. Express the remaining value as -4.

**Final Answer: -4.**

**Verification:** |-9| = 9 and |+5| = 5. Their magnitude difference is 4, and the negative addend has the greater absolute value.

### 2.5 Adding Opposite Integers

Opposite integers have equal absolute values and opposite signs. When represented using counters, they produce equal numbers of positive and negative units.

For example, (+6) + (-6) produces six zero pairs. No unmatched counters remain, so the sum is zero.

This relationship is expressed by the additive inverse principle.

> **FORMULA CARD - ADDITIVE INVERSE**
>
> **a + (-a) = 0**
>
> Where:
>
> - a = any integer
> - -a = the opposite of a
> - 0 = the sum of an integer and its opposite

The equation is valid for every integer, including zero.

## SECTION 3. ADDING INTEGERS USING NUMBER LINES

### 3.1 The Number Line as a Movement Model

A number line represents integer addition through movement from an initial position. The first addend identifies the starting value, while the second addend determines the direction and distance of movement.

Adding a positive integer means moving to the right on a horizontally oriented number line. Adding a negative integer means moving to the left. These movements follow the standard numerical ordering in which values increase toward the right and decrease toward the left.

For example, (+2) + (+5) begins at +2 and moves five unit intervals to the right, arriving at +7. The final position represents the sum.

The expression (+2) + (-5) begins at +2 but moves five unit intervals to the left, arriving at -3. Although the operation is addition, the direction of movement is negative because the second addend is negative.

The starting position must be distinguished from the movement represented by the second addend. For example, (-4) + (+6) begins at -4 and moves six units to the right. The result is +2. Beginning at zero and moving only six units would ignore the first addend and therefore misrepresent the expression.

Number-line representations connect arithmetic with spatial reasoning. The final position provides a visual method of checking the result, while the direction of movement explains the effect of the sign attached to the second addend.

### 3.3 Worked Example - Crossing Zero

**Problem:** Evaluate (-6) + (+9) using a number line.

**Given:** Starting integer = -6; second addend = +9.

**Find:** The final position and sum.

**Principle:** Adding a positive integer corresponds to rightward movement.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Draw a number line extending from at least -7 to +9.
2. Mark the starting integer -6.
3. Identify the direction of movement as right because the second addend is +9.
4. Move nine equal unit intervals to the right, crossing zero after six intervals and continuing three more intervals.
5. Identify the final position as +3.

### 3.4 Adding a Negative Integer to a Positive Integer

Consider (+8) + (-11). The number line begins at +8, and the addition of -11 requires movement eleven intervals to the left.

(+8) + (-11) = -3.

The result can also be interpreted as the difference between magnitudes 11 and 8, with the sign of the greater-magnitude addend.

This geometric model helps explain why adding a negative integer may reduce a positive value, move the result across zero, or produce a negative sum.

### 3.5 Connection Between Counter and Number-Line Models

Counters and number lines represent the same mathematical relationship through different structures.

For (+5) + (-8), the counter model leaves three negative counters after removing five zero pairs. The number-line model begins at +5 and moves eight units left to -3.

The agreement between the two models supports the symbolic rule that different-sign integer addition is based on comparing absolute values and retaining the sign of the addend with greater magnitude.

## SECTION 4. RULES FOR ADDING INTEGERS AND WORKED EXAMPLES

### 4.1 Deriving the Same-Sign Rule

The concrete counter model demonstrates that two positive collections combine into a larger positive collection. Similarly, two negative collections combine into a larger negative collection.

The corresponding symbolic rule is:

> **FORMULA CARD - ADDITION OF INTEGERS WITH THE SAME SIGN**
>
> **Add the absolute values and retain the common sign.**
>
> For positive integers a and b:
>
> **(+a) + (+b) = +(a + b)**
>
> **(-a) + (-b) = -(a + b)**
>
> Where:
>
> - a and b represent positive whole-number magnitudes
> - the signs identify the common direction of the addends

Examples:

(+7) + (+9) = +16

(-7) + (-9) = -16

(-15) + (-6) = -21

### 4.2 Deriving the Different-Sign Rule

The zero-pair model demonstrates that positive and negative counters cancel in matching pairs.

When the absolute values of two addends differ, some counters remain after cancellation. The number of remaining counters equals the difference between the two absolute values.

> **FORMULA CARD - ADDITION OF INTEGERS WITH DIFFERENT SIGNS**
>
> **Subtract the smaller absolute value from the larger absolute value.**
>
> **Give the nonzero result the sign of the addend with the greater absolute value.**
>
> If the absolute values are equal, the sum is zero.
>
> Here, absolute value refers to the nonnegative distance of a number from zero.

Examples:

(+12) + (-5) = +7

(-12) + (+5) = -7

(+8) + (-8) = 0

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3C - SAME-SIGN ADDITION

**Problem:** Calculate (-18) + (-27).

**Given:** Integers -18 and -27.

**Find:** Their sum.

**Formula/Principle:** Add absolute values and retain the common negative sign.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the signs: both addends are negative.
2. Determine absolute values: |-18| = 18 and |-27| = 27.
3. Add the absolute values: 18 + 27 = 45.
4. Retain the negative sign because both original addends are negative.
5. Verify that the negative result has magnitude 45.

**Final Answer: (-18) + (-27) = -45.**

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3D - DIFFERENT-SIGN ADDITION

**Problem:** Calculate (+35) + (-48).

**Given:** Integers +35 and -48.

**Find:** Their sum.

**Formula/Principle:** Subtract absolute values and retain the sign of the addend with greater magnitude.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify that the signs are different.
2. Determine the absolute values: 35 and 48.
3. Subtract the smaller magnitude from the larger: 48 - 35 = 13.
4. Identify the greater-magnitude addend: -48.
5. Assign the negative sign to the magnitude difference.

**Final Answer: (+35) + (-48) = -13.**

**Verification:** Thirteen negative units remain after 35 positive units cancel with 35 of the 48 negative units.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.3E - ADDING THREE INTEGERS

**Problem:** Evaluate (-12) + (+7) + (+15).

**Given:** -12, +7, and +15.

**Find:** Their sum.

**Principle:** Integer addition may be grouped without changing the result.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Write the expression: (-12) + (+7) + (+15).
2. Group the positive integers: (-12) + (7 + 15).
3. Add the positive values: 7 + 15 = 22.
4. Combine integers of different signs: -12 + 22 = +10.
5. Verify by sequential addition: -12 + 7 = -5; -5 + 15 = 10.

## SECTION 5. MATHEMATICAL PROPERTIES AND COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### 5.1 Commutative Property of Addition

The **commutative property** states that changing the order of two addends does not change their sum.

> **FORMULA CARD - COMMUTATIVE PROPERTY**
>
> **a + b = b + a**
>
> Where a and b are integers.

For example:

(-8) + (+3) = -5

(+3) + (-8) = -5

The property can be justified using counters because combining two collections produces the same total regardless of which collection is placed first.

### 5.2 Associative Property of Addition

The **associative property** states that regrouping three integers does not change their sum.

> **FORMULA CARD - ASSOCIATIVE PROPERTY**
>
> **(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)**
>
> Where a, b, and c are integers.

Consider:

(-6 + 4) + 9 = -2 + 9 = 7

-6 + (4 + 9) = -6 + 13 = 7

Both groupings produce the same sum.

The property is particularly useful when several integers must be added. Grouping convenient combinations can reduce calculation errors.

### 5.3 Identity Property of Addition

Zero is the **additive identity** because adding zero does not change the value of an integer.

> **FORMULA CARD - ADDITIVE IDENTITY**
>
> **a + 0 = 0 + a = a**

Examples:

-14 + 0 = -14

0 + 23 = 23

0 + 0 = 0

### 5.4 Additive Inverse Property

The additive inverse property states that an integer and its opposite sum to zero.

For example:

-19 + 19 = 0

28 + (-28) = 0

This property explains why zero pairs are mathematically valid.

### 5.5 Closure Property

The **closure property** states that the sum of two integers is always an integer.

For example:

-7 + 12 = 5

-20 + (-8) = -28

Both results belong to ℤ.

Closure is important because it confirms that integer addition does not produce a non-integer value when both addends are integers.

### 5.6 Common Misconceptions and Error Analysis

| Incorrect Reasoning | Why It Is Incorrect | Correct Solution |
|---|---|---|
| -6 + (-4) = +10 | Two negative addends produce a negative sum | -6 + (-4) = -10 |
| +9 + (-5) = -4 | The positive addend has greater magnitude | +9 + (-5) = +4 |
| -13 + 7 = -20 | Different signs require magnitude comparison | -13 + 7 = -6 |
| -8 + 8 = 16 | Opposite integers cancel | -8 + 8 = 0 |
| -5 + 0 = 0 | Zero is the additive identity | -5 + 0 = -5 |
| 6 + (-11) = +5 | The negative magnitude is larger | 6 + (-11) = -5 |

### Mathematical Reasoning: Why Adding a Negative Moves Left

Consider 4 + (-3).

By the additive inverse property, 3 + (-3) = 0.

Therefore:

4 + (-3) = 1 + 3 + (-3)

Using the associative property:

= 1 + [3 + (-3)]

= 1 + 0

### Background: Understanding Subtraction Beyond Whole Numbers

Subtraction is a fundamental mathematical operation used to determine differences, changes, and unknown quantities. In elementary mathematics, subtraction is often introduced through the removal of objects from a collection. For example, removing three pencils from a collection of eight leaves five pencils. This concrete interpretation establishes an important foundation, but it is not sufficient for explaining every subtraction involving negative integers.

Consider a student who has ₱200 but owes a classmate ₱350. A signed balance model represents the situation as 200 - 350 = -150. The negative result communicates that the obligation exceeds the available amount by ₱150. Such situations demonstrate why subtraction must extend beyond whole-number answers.

The same extension is necessary when subtracting negative integers. An expression such as 6 - (-4) may initially appear difficult because the number being subtracted is already negative. Removing a negative quantity is different from removing a positive quantity. The operation must be interpreted mathematically rather than by applying an informal rule about two negative symbols.

The key principle is that **subtracting an integer is equivalent to adding its opposite**. For example, 6 - (-4) is equivalent to 6 + 4, producing a result of 10. This rule is not merely a computational shortcut. It follows from the relationship between addition and subtraction and can be demonstrated using counters, zero pairs, and number-line movements.

The concept of additive inverses, introduced in the preceding lessons, provides a foundation for this relationship. An integer and its opposite combine to produce zero. The integers 5 and -5 are additive inverses because 5 + (-5) = 0. Likewise, -9 and 9 are additive inverses because -9 + 9 = 0.

Using integer counters, learners can represent positive and negative values concretely. When a subtraction requires the removal of counters that are not present in the starting collection, zero pairs can be introduced. Because each zero pair has a total value of zero, adding such pairs does not change the original value of the collection.

Number lines provide another model. Subtracting a positive integer corresponds to movement toward the left, while subtracting a negative integer corresponds to movement toward the right. This directional interpretation agrees with the principle of adding the opposite.

The relationship between addition and subtraction also allows solutions to be checked. If a - b = c, then c + b must equal a. This inverse-operation property helps learners identify mistakes and justify their answers.

In this lesson, subtraction is developed systematically through concrete manipulation, visual representation, algebraic reasoning, and contextual applications. The emphasis is not limited to obtaining correct numerical answers. Learners must also explain why procedures work and how numerical signs communicate meaningful relationships.

## SECTION 2. SUBTRACTING INTEGERS USING COUNTERS AND ZERO PAIRS

### 2.1 Integer Counters as a Concrete Subtraction Model

Integer counters provide a concrete representation of signed values. For consistency, blue counters will represent +1 and orange counters will represent -1. A collection of five blue counters therefore has a value of +5, while a collection of seven orange counters has a value of -7.

In a subtraction model, the first integer represents the starting collection. The second integer represents the signed quantity that must be removed. The value remaining after the specified removal represents the difference.

For example, 7 - 3 can be modeled by starting with seven positive counters and removing three positive counters. Four positive counters remain, so the difference is +4. This model closely resembles familiar whole-number subtraction.

However, the operation 3 - 7 cannot be performed by removing seven positive counters from an initial collection containing only three positive counters. The starting collection does not have enough positive counters to remove. The mathematical problem is resolved by introducing zero pairs.

A **zero pair** consists of one positive counter and one negative counter. Because +1 + (-1) = 0, adding a zero pair does not change the value of a collection. Therefore, three positive counters and four zero pairs still represent the integer +3.

After the four zero pairs are added, the collection contains seven positive counters and four negative counters. Removing seven positive counters leaves four negative counters. The resulting value is -4, demonstrating that 3 - 7 = -4.

This model explains why subtraction may produce a negative answer even when the starting integer is positive. The negative result is not an error or an impossible quantity. It is the value remaining after the specified positive counters have been removed from an equivalent representation of the original integer.

### 2.2 The Role of Zero Pairs

A zero pair has a combined numerical value of zero. Adding or removing a complete zero pair does not change the represented integer. This relationship is an application of the additive inverse property.

> **FORMULA CARD - ZERO-PAIR PRINCIPLE**
>
> **(+1) + (-1) = 0**
>
> **a + 0 = a**
>
> Where:
>
> - +1 represents one positive counter.
> - -1 represents one negative counter.
> - a represents any integer.
>
> Introducing an equal number of positive and negative counters preserves the total value of the collection.

Suppose five negative counters represent -5. Adding three zero pairs produces three positive counters and eight negative counters. The total value remains -5 because the added counters contribute zero.

The expanded collection can be used to subtract three positive counters. After those counters are removed, eight negative counters remain. Thus, -5 - 3 = -8.

This construction establishes an important connection between concrete manipulation and symbolic subtraction.

**Problem:** Evaluate (+3) - (-4) using integer counters.

**Find:** The difference.

### 2.6 Deriving the General Principle from Counters

The counter models reveal that subtracting a positive quantity has the same effect as adding an equal negative quantity. Similarly, subtracting a negative quantity has the same effect as adding an equal positive quantity.

Consider 5 - 2. Removing two positive counters from five positive counters leaves three positive counters. The same result is obtained by starting with five positive counters, adding two negative counters, and canceling two zero pairs.

Both procedures yield +3.

Now consider 5 - (-2). Introducing two zero pairs makes it possible to remove two negative counters. The remaining seven positive counters represent +7. The result agrees with adding the positive opposite of -2:

5 + 2 = 7.

The concrete models therefore establish the rule of subtracting by adding the opposite.

## SECTION 3. SUBTRACTION AS ADDITION OF THE OPPOSITE

### 3.1 The Relationship Between Addition and Subtraction

Subtraction and addition are inverse operations. If adding a particular number produces a new value, subtracting that same number reverses the change.

For example, 6 + 4 = 10. The related subtraction equation is 10 - 4 = 6. The operations are connected because each can be used to recover an unknown value in the other.

This relationship remains valid when integers are negative. If -3 + 7 = 4, then 4 - 7 = -3.

To develop a general subtraction rule, it is necessary to identify the additive inverse of the second integer. The additive inverse of +5 is -5, while the additive inverse of -5 is +5.

The mathematical principle states that subtracting an integer is equivalent to adding its additive inverse. The first integer remains unchanged, while the subtraction operation and the sign of the second integer are transformed together.

> **FORMULA CARD - SUBTRACTION OF INTEGERS**
>
> **a - b = a + (-b)**
>
> Where:
>
> - a = the minuend, or integer from which a quantity is subtracted
> - b = the subtrahend, or integer being subtracted
> - -b = the additive inverse of b
> - a - b = the difference
>
> **Procedure:** Keep the first integer, change subtraction to addition, and replace the second integer with its opposite.

This rule applies to every pair of integers.

### 3.2 Subtracting Positive Integers

When the second integer is positive, its additive inverse is negative.

For example:

8 - 3 = 8 + (-3) = 5.

Similarly:

-6 - 4 = -6 + (-4) = -10.

The first example reduces a positive value. The second combines two negative quantities after subtraction is rewritten as addition.

### 3.3 Subtracting Negative Integers

When the second integer is negative, its additive inverse is positive.

For example:

7 - (-5) = 7 + 5 = 12.

Likewise:

-8 - (-3) = -8 + 3 = -5.

The second example is particularly important because subtracting a negative number does not always produce a positive answer. Although subtracting -3 changes the result in the positive direction, the starting value -8 is sufficiently negative that the final answer remains negative.

The rule determines the change in the expression, not an automatic positive sign for the final result.

### 3.5 Worked Example - Applying the Additive Inverse

**Problem:** Evaluate (-15) - (-28).

**Given:** a = -15; b = -28.

**Find:** The difference.

**Formula:** a - b = a + (-b).

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Write the original expression: (-15) - (-28).
2. Determine the opposite of -28, which is +28.
3. Rewrite subtraction as addition: (-15) + (+28).
4. Compare absolute values and subtract: 28 - 15 = 13.
5. Retain the positive sign because +28 has the greater absolute value.

**Final Answer: (-15) - (-28) = +13.**

**Verification:** 13 + (-28) = -15.

### 3.6 Mathematical Derivation

To establish why a - b = a + (-b), consider a number x that satisfies the equation:

x + b = a.

The value x is the difference a - b because adding b to the difference gives a.

Now consider:

a + (-b) + b.

Using the associative property:

= a + [(-b) + b].

Since b and -b are additive inverses:

= a + 0.

Therefore:

= a.

Thus, a + (-b) satisfies the same defining relationship as a - b. Consequently:

**a - b = a + (-b).**

This explanation justifies the subtraction rule through addition properties rather than relying only on memorization.

### 3.7 The Difference Between a Negative Sign and a Subtraction Symbol

The symbol - has two important mathematical functions.

In the expression -8, it indicates a negative integer or the opposite of +8.

In the expression 12 - 8, it represents the operation of subtraction.

In 12 - (-8), the first minus symbol indicates subtraction, while the second indicates that the subtrahend is negative.

Parentheses make the distinction visible and reduce ambiguity.

A reliable computation begins by identifying which minus symbol is an operation and which is part of a signed value.

## SECTION 4. NUMBER-LINE REPRESENTATIONS AND MATHEMATICAL VERIFICATION

### 4.1 Directional Movement on a Number Line

A number line represents integers as points arranged in increasing order from left to right. Subtraction can be modeled by movement from the position of the first integer.

When subtracting a positive integer, the movement is toward the left. When subtracting a negative integer, the movement is toward the right. These movements agree with adding the opposite because the direction is determined by the sign of the equivalent addend.

For example, 4 - 6 begins at +4 and moves six units left. After four intervals, the movement reaches zero. Two additional intervals lead to -2. Therefore:

4 - 6 = -2.

In contrast, 4 - (-6) begins at +4 and moves six units right. The movement ends at +10.

Although the expressions contain the same absolute values, they produce different results because the signs and operations differ.

### 4.3 Worked Example - Subtracting Across Zero

**Problem:** Evaluate (-3) - (+5) using a number line.

**Given:** Starting integer = -3; subtrahend = +5.

**Find:** The difference.

**Principle:** Subtracting a positive integer corresponds to leftward movement.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Draw a number line extending from at least -9 to +2.
2. Locate the starting value -3.
3. Identify the movement as five units left because the subtrahend is +5.
4. Count the five intervals: -4, -5, -6, -7, and -8.
5. Identify the final position as -8.

**Final Answer: (-3) - (+5) = -8.**

**Verification:** -8 + 5 = -3.

### 4.4 Worked Example - Subtracting a Negative Across Zero

**Problem:** Evaluate (-4) - (-9) using a number line.

**Given:** Starting integer = -4; subtrahend = -9.

**Find:** The difference.

**Principle:** Subtracting a negative integer corresponds to rightward movement.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Locate -4 on a horizontal number line.
2. Identify the opposite of -9 as +9.
3. Rewrite the expression: (-4) + (+9).
4. Move nine intervals to the right from -4, passing through zero and stopping at +5.
5. Verify that the final value satisfies 5 + (-9) = -4.

**Final Answer: (-4) - (-9) = +5.**

### 4.5 Subtraction as a Difference Between Positions

Subtraction can also determine the signed change between two positions. If a person moves from an initial coordinate a to a final coordinate b, the signed change is b - a.

Suppose the temperature changes from -6°C to +4°C. The final reading minus the initial reading is:

4 - (-6) = 4 + 6 = +10°C.

The positive result means that the temperature increased by 10 Celsius degrees.

A different question asks for the distance between two positions. The distance is the absolute value of their difference. For positions -6 and 4, the distance is |4 - (-6)| = 10 units.

Signed change and distance are related, but they must be interpreted differently. Signed change communicates direction, while distance is nonnegative.

### 4.6 Verification Using the Inverse Operation

Subtraction results should be checked through addition.

If a - b = c, then the relationship c + b = a must hold.

> **FORMULA CARD - SUBTRACTION CHECK**
>
> **a - b = c if and only if c + b = a**
>
> Where:
>
> - a = minuend
> - b = subtrahend
> - c = difference
>
> The verification restores the original minuend by adding the subtrahend to the calculated difference.

For example:

-14 - (-8) = -6.

Verification:

-6 + (-8) = -14.

Since the original minuend is recovered, the subtraction is correct.

This method is particularly useful for expressions containing two negative signs, where sign errors commonly occur.

## SECTION 5. MATHEMATICAL REASONING AND COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### 5.1 Subtraction Is Not Commutative

The commutative property applies to integer addition but not generally to subtraction.

For example:

8 - 3 = 5.

Reversing the order gives:

3 - 8 = -5.

The two results are different. Therefore:

8 - 3 ≠ 3 - 8.

Changing the order of the minuend and subtrahend changes the mathematical operation being performed. Learners must preserve the original order of subtraction unless a valid transformation is applied.

### 5.2 Subtraction Is Not Associative

The associative property also does not generally apply to subtraction.

Consider:

(12 - 5) - 3 = 7 - 3 = 4.

Now consider:

12 - (5 - 3) = 12 - 2 = 10.

Since 4 ≠ 10, the two groupings are not equivalent.

Parentheses therefore determine which subtraction must be performed first. Ignoring them can change the value of an expression.

When simplifying more complicated expressions, subtraction can be rewritten as addition of opposites. This permits legitimate use of addition properties without treating subtraction itself as associative.

For example:

12 - 5 - 3 = 12 + (-5) + (-3) = 4.

The rewritten expression can be regrouped using the associative property of addition.

### 5.3 Subtracting Zero

Subtracting zero does not change the value of an integer.

For every integer a:

a - 0 = a.

Examples:

-11 - 0 = -11.

18 - 0 = 18.

0 - 0 = 0.

However, zero minus an integer produces the opposite of that integer:

0 - 7 = -7.

0 - (-7) = +7.

These expressions show why the position of zero in a subtraction problem matters.

### 5.4 Subtracting an Integer from Itself

Every integer subtracted from itself gives zero.

a - a = 0.

For example:

-12 - (-12) = 0.

The same relationship can be verified through adding the opposite:

-12 + 12 = 0.

The result follows from the additive inverse property.

### 5.5 Error-Analysis Matrix

| Incorrect Solution | Mathematical Error | Correct Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| 7 - (-4) = 3 | Treats a negative subtrahend as positive | 7 + 4 = 11 |
| -6 - 5 = -1 | Moves right rather than left | -6 + (-5) = -11 |
| -8 - (-3) = -11 | Fails to change the second integer to its opposite | -8 + 3 = -5 |
| 4 - 9 = 5 | Ignores the sign of the difference | 4 + (-9) = -5 |
| -10 - (-10) = -20 | Subtracts magnitudes without considering opposites | -10 + 10 = 0 |
| 0 - (-6) = -6 | Incorrectly retains the negative sign of the subtrahend | 0 + 6 = 6 |

### 5.6 Watch Out: Two Negative Signs

A common mistake is to assume that any expression containing two minus signs must have a positive final answer.

Consider:

-15 - (-4).

The expression becomes:

-15 + 4 = -11.

The subtraction of a negative number is transformed into addition of a positive number, but the resulting sum remains negative.

The correct rule is not that two negative signs always give a positive answer. The correct rule is that subtracting a negative integer means adding its positive opposite. The final sign depends on the resulting integer sum.

### 5.7 Error Investigation

A learner calculates:

-18 - (-25) = -43.

The learner explains that the answer is negative because both integers are negative.

This reasoning incorrectly applies the same-sign addition rule directly to a subtraction expression.

A correct solution first replaces subtraction by addition of the opposite:

-18 - (-25) = -18 + 25.

The absolute value of +25 is greater than that of -18. The difference in magnitudes is 7.

Therefore, the result is +7.

Verification:

7 + (-25) = -18.

The verified answer is +7, not -43.

## SECTION 6. APPLIED MASTERY - INTEGER SUBTRACTION IN REAL-WORLD SITUATIONS

### 6.1 Financial Balances and Transactions

Signed financial quantities can represent balances, credits, expenses, and obligations. When a person has a positive balance, the value is greater than zero. A negative balance may indicate debt or an amount owed. The meaning depends on the financial model, but subtraction must always follow the established sign convention.

Suppose a student has ₱350 available and spends ₱480. The resulting balance can be represented by:

350 - 480 = -130.

The result indicates a deficit of ₱130, assuming the financial arrangement permits spending beyond the amount available.

Now suppose a student owes ₱250 but receives a debt adjustment that cancels ₱100 of the obligation. The balance can be modeled as:

-250 - (-100) = -150.

Subtracting a negative obligation improves the balance because part of the debt has been removed.

### 6.2 Changes in Temperature

Temperature comparisons frequently involve values above and below zero. Subtraction determines the difference between an initial temperature and a final temperature.

Consider a temperature that increases from -5°C to +7°C.

The signed change is:

7 - (-5) = 12°C.

The temperature increased by 12 Celsius degrees.

If the temperature instead changes from +7°C to -5°C, the signed change is:

-5 - 7 = -12°C.

The negative result indicates a decrease of 12 Celsius degrees.

Although both situations involve the same temperatures, the direction of change depends on which measurement is initial and which is final.

### 6.3 Comparing Elevations

Signed elevation measurements describe positions relative to sea level or another reference. Subtracting an initial elevation from a final elevation determines the signed vertical displacement.

Suppose an observation station is located at -80 m and a second station is located at +120 m.

The upward displacement from the first station to the second is:

120 - (-80) = 200 m.

The result indicates an upward change of 200 m.

The reverse movement has signed displacement:

-80 - 120 = -200 m.

Both movements cover the same vertical distance, but they occur in opposite directions.

### 6.4 Calculating Score Differences

Subtraction can compare scores or score changes. A signed score of -4 may represent a penalty or a net loss, while a positive value indicates a gain.

Suppose Team A has a net score adjustment of +12 points and Team B has -5 points.

The difference between their adjustments is:

12 - (-5) = 17 points.

Team A's score adjustment is 17 points greater than Team B's.

The example shows why subtracting a negative value is necessary when comparing a gain against a loss.

### Background: Understanding Multiplication Beyond Positive Numbers

Multiplication is a fundamental arithmetic operation used to represent equal groups, repeated quantities, scaling, and numerical relationships. In elementary mathematics, learners commonly encounter multiplication through positive quantities. For example, four groups containing three objects each are represented by 4 × 3 = 12. This expression can also be interpreted as repeated addition: 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.

As learners progress into the set of integers, multiplication must accommodate both positive and negative numerical values. Negative integers can represent quantities in opposite directions, including financial losses, decreases in temperature, movement below a reference level, and negative changes in measured quantities. Multiplication extends these representations by describing repeated signed changes and relationships between signed factors.

Consider a school organization that spends ₱75 each day for four consecutive days. If an expense is represented as a negative change in the organization's cash balance, each transaction has the signed value -75. The combined change can be expressed as (-75) + (-75) + (-75) + (-75). Using multiplication, the same relationship is represented as 4 × (-75) = -300. The negative product indicates a total decrease of ₱300.

The example establishes that multiplying a positive number by a negative integer can be understood as repeated addition of a negative quantity. However, this interpretation alone does not fully explain every combination of signs. In particular, multiplying a negative integer by another negative integer requires mathematical reasoning involving opposites, numerical patterns, and the distributive property.

Integer multiplication is governed by consistent sign rules. The product of two positive integers is positive. The product of one positive and one negative integer is negative. The product of two negative integers is positive. If either factor is zero, the product is zero.

These sign rules are not independent procedures to be memorized without explanation. They follow from the properties of arithmetic and ensure that integer multiplication remains consistent with addition, subtraction, and the distributive property. Understanding their mathematical basis enables learners to use the rules accurately even in more complex expressions.

Multiplication also involves important structural properties. The commutative property allows factors to exchange positions without changing the product. The associative property permits regrouping. The distributive property connects multiplication with addition and subtraction. The multiplicative identity property explains why multiplying by one preserves a number, while the zero property explains why any integer multiplied by zero produces zero.

The lesson develops these concepts through concrete and pictorial models, number patterns, mathematical derivations, worked examples, and practical investigations. Learners are expected not only to calculate correctly but also to interpret signs, verify numerical results, and communicate the reasoning supporting their conclusions.

## SECTION 2. MULTIPLICATION AS REPEATED ADDITION AND EQUAL GROUPS

### 2.1 Repeated Addition of Positive Integers

Repeated addition provides an introductory model of multiplication. When an equal quantity is added several times, multiplication expresses the operation more efficiently. For example, five groups of four positive counters contain twenty counters. The corresponding numerical expression is 5 × 4 = 20.

The first factor may represent the number of equal groups, while the second factor represents the value in each group. This interpretation is useful when the number of groups is a nonnegative whole number. The product represents the total value obtained by combining all groups.

A mathematical model should distinguish the physical number of groups from the signed value represented by each group. A positive number of groups is a count and therefore cannot represent a negative number of physical groups. This distinction becomes essential when extending multiplication to negative factors.

### 2.2 Repeated Addition of Negative Integers

Repeated addition can also represent multiplication involving a positive number of groups containing negative quantities.

Consider four groups of three negative counters. Each group represents -3. Combining the groups produces:

(-3) + (-3) + (-3) + (-3) = -12.

The equivalent multiplication expression is:

4 × (-3) = -12.

This result follows naturally because twelve negative counters are present. No positive counters are available to form zero pairs, so the total value remains negative.

In financial contexts, four repeated expenses of ₱3 each produce a total change of -₱12. In a points-based activity, losing three points in each of four rounds produces a total change of -12 points.

The repeated-addition model therefore provides a concrete explanation for a positive integer multiplied by a negative integer.

### 2.3 Interpreting Negative Factors

A negative factor cannot always be interpreted as a literal negative number of physical groups. Instead, its sign may represent a reversal, an opposite, or a relationship involving signed changes.

For example, -3 × 4 is the opposite of 3 × 4. Since 3 × 4 = 12, the corresponding negative product is -12.

This interpretation agrees with the commutative property:

-3 × 4 = 4 × (-3) = -12.

For the multiplication of two negative integers, a more complete explanation is provided through the distributive property and numerical patterns. These establish why the product must be positive.

### 2.4 Concrete Counter Representation

Suppose a learner uses orange counters to represent negative units.

Five groups, each containing four negative counters, model the expression 5 × (-4).

The complete collection contains twenty negative counters:

5 × (-4) = -20.

The counter model emphasizes two components of multiplication: the number of repetitions and the signed value repeated.

A corresponding positive model contains five groups of four positive counters:

5 × (+4) = +20.

The two products have equal absolute values because their group counts and magnitudes match. Their signs differ because one model combines positive quantities and the other combines negative quantities.

## SECTION 3. SIGN RULES AND NUMERICAL PATTERNS

### 3.1 Multiplying Two Positive Integers

The product of two positive integers is positive. This result agrees with multiplication as repeated addition of positive quantities.

For example:

(+4) × (+6) = +24.

Four groups of six positive units form twenty-four positive units.

The positive-positive sign rule is familiar from earlier arithmetic and remains valid in the larger integer system.

### 3.2 Multiplying Integers with Different Signs

When exactly one factor is negative and the other is positive, the product is negative.

For example:

(+5) × (-4) = -20.

This expression represents five repetitions of -4.

The corresponding reversed order has the same product:

(-4) × (+5) = -20.

The commutative property guarantees that changing the order of factors does not change the product.

The negative sign indicates that the product lies below zero, while the magnitude is obtained by multiplying the absolute values of the factors.

### 3.3 Multiplying Two Negative Integers

The product of two negative integers is positive. This relationship can be investigated by extending familiar multiplication patterns.

Consider the sequence:

3 × (-4) = -12  
2 × (-4) = -8  
1 × (-4) = -4  
0 × (-4) = 0

As the first factor decreases by one, the product increases by four. Continuing the pattern gives:

(-1) × (-4) = +4  
(-2) × (-4) = +8  
(-3) × (-4) = +12

The pattern remains consistent with the structure of integer multiplication. Therefore:

**(-3) × (-4) = +12.**

The positive product is not based solely on a memorized statement that two negative signs cancel. It follows from preserving the relationships established by multiplication and addition.

### 3.4 Complete Sign Rules

> **FORMULA CARD - INTEGER MULTIPLICATION SIGN RULES**
>
> **Positive × Positive = Positive**
>
> **Positive × Negative = Negative**
>
> **Negative × Positive = Negative**
>
> **Negative × Negative = Positive**
>
> **Any Integer × 0 = 0**
>
> To multiply two nonzero integers:
>
> 1. Determine the signs of the factors.
> 2. Multiply their absolute values.
> 3. Assign the correct sign to the product.
>
> When both factors have the same sign, the product is positive. When the signs differ, the product is negative.

Multiplication by zero always produces zero, regardless of the sign of the other factor.

The result is neither positive nor negative. Zero is the only integer that is neither positive nor negative.

## SECTION 4. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION AND COMPLETE WORKED EXAMPLES

### 4.1 Why a Negative Times a Negative Is Positive

The distributive property establishes that multiplication must remain consistent with addition.

Consider:

0 = (-3) × 0.

Since 0 = 4 + (-4), substitute:

0 = (-3) × [4 + (-4)].

Applying the distributive property:

0 = [(-3) × 4] + [(-3) × (-4)].

The first product is -12 because multiplying a negative integer by a positive integer produces a negative result.

Therefore:

0 = -12 + [(-3) × (-4)].

The number that must be added to -12 to obtain zero is +12.

Thus:

**(-3) × (-4) = +12.**

This derivation establishes the positive product through mathematical consistency. The result is necessary for the distributive property and the additive inverse relationship to hold.

### 4.2 A General Derivation

Let a and b be positive integers.

Because b + (-b) = 0:

(-a) × [b + (-b)] = (-a) × 0.

The right side equals zero. Applying the distributive property to the left side gives:

[(-a) × b] + [(-a) × (-b)] = 0.

The first product is -ab, so:

-ab + [(-a) × (-b)] = 0.

Therefore, the second product must equal +ab:

**(-a) × (-b) = ab.**

This relationship demonstrates that the multiplication of two negative integers produces a positive result.

### 4.3 Multiplying Three or More Integers

When multiplying more than two integers, the sign can be determined by counting the negative factors, provided that none of the factors is zero.

An even number of negative factors produces a positive product. An odd number of negative factors produces a negative product.

For example:

(-2) × (-3) × (-4)

= (+6) × (-4)

= -24.

Three negative factors produce a negative result.

Consider:

(-2) × (-3) × (-4) × (-5).

First:

(-2) × (-3) = +6.

Then:

(+6) × (-4) = -24.

Finally:

(-24) × (-5) = +120.

Four negative factors produce a positive result.

If any factor is zero, the entire product is zero, regardless of the other factors.

> **FORMULA CARD - SIGN OF MULTIPLE FACTORS**
>
> For a product containing no zero factors:
>
> **Even number of negative factors → Positive product**
>
> **Odd number of negative factors → Negative product**
>
> If at least one factor is zero:
>
> **Product = 0**
>
> The magnitude is found by multiplying the absolute values of all factors.

### 4.4 Mathematical Reasoning with Opposites

The multiplicative effect of -1 is especially important.

Multiplying an integer by -1 produces its opposite.

For example:

(-1) × (+7) = -7.

(-1) × (-7) = +7.

The relationship is expressed as:

**(-1) × a = -a.**

Where a is any integer.

This property explains why multiplying by -1 reverses the sign of a nonzero value. Applying the operation twice returns the original value:

(-1) × [(-1) × a] = a.

For instance:

(-1) × [(-1) × 8]

= (-1) × (-8)

= +8.

The same relationship holds for negative starting values.

### 4.5 Error Analysis in Multiple-Factor Multiplication

Consider the incorrect solution:

(-3) × (-4) × (-2) = +24.

The learner may have assumed that the presence of two negative signs automatically makes the entire expression positive.

However, the expression contains three negative factors, not two. The first two factors produce +12, but multiplying that result by -2 produces -24.

The correct procedure is:

(-3) × (-4) = +12.

(+12) × (-2) = -24.

Therefore:

**(-3) × (-4) × (-2) = -24.**

The sign must be determined from all factors in the expression.

## SECTION 5. MATHEMATICAL PROPERTIES AND COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### 5.1 Commutative Property

The commutative property states that the order of two factors does not affect their product.

> **FORMULA CARD - COMMUTATIVE PROPERTY**
>
> **a × b = b × a**
>
> Where a and b are integers.

For example:

(-7) × (+4) = -28.

(+4) × (-7) = -28.

Both arrangements produce the same result.

This property is useful when changing the order makes a multiplication expression easier to compute.

### 5.2 Associative Property

The associative property states that regrouping three factors does not affect their product.

> **FORMULA CARD - ASSOCIATIVE PROPERTY**
>
> **(a × b) × c = a × (b × c)**

For example:

[(-2) × (+5)] × (-3)

= (-10) × (-3)

= +30.

Regrouping gives:

(-2) × [(+5) × (-3)]

= (-2) × (-15)

= +30.

Both groupings produce the same product.

### 5.3 Distributive Property

The distributive property connects multiplication with addition.

> **FORMULA CARD - DISTRIBUTIVE PROPERTY**
>
> **a × (b + c) = (a × b) + (a × c)**

For example:

(-3) × (5 + 2)

= (-3) × 7

= -21.

Using the distributive property:

[(-3) × 5] + [(-3) × 2]

= -15 + (-6)

= -21.

The two methods agree.

The property also applies when one or more terms inside the parentheses are negative.

### 5.4 Multiplicative Identity

The integer 1 is the multiplicative identity.

> **FORMULA CARD - MULTIPLICATIVE IDENTITY**
>
> **a × 1 = 1 × a = a**

Examples:

-16 × 1 = -16.

1 × 25 = 25.

Multiplying by one preserves both the magnitude and sign of an integer.

### 5.5 Zero Property

For every integer a:

**a × 0 = 0 × a = 0.**

Examples:

-25 × 0 = 0.

0 × 73 = 0.

The zero property must be checked before applying a rule about an even or odd number of negative factors.

### 5.6 Closure Property

The product of any two integers is another integer.

Examples:

(-6) × (+8) = -48.

(-6) × (-8) = +48.

Both products belong to the set of integers.

### 5.7 Error-Analysis Matrix

| Incorrect Reasoning | Mathematical Error | Correct Result |
|---|---|---|
| (-7) × (-5) = -35 | Treats same negative signs as producing a negative product | +35 |
| (+8) × (-4) = +32 | Ignores the different-sign rule | -32 |
| (-12) × 0 = -12 | Does not apply the zero property | 0 |
| (-1) × (-16) = -16 | Does not recognize multiplication by -1 as taking the opposite | +16 |
| (-2) × (-3) × (-4) = +24 | Ignores the third negative factor | -24 |
| (-5) × (3 + 2) = -15 + 2 | Does not multiply both terms by -5 | -25 |

### 5.8 Mathematical Reasoning: Sign and Magnitude

A product has two features that should be analyzed separately when dealing with nonzero integers: its sign and its magnitude.

The sign depends on the signs of the factors. The magnitude depends on the product of their absolute values.

For example:

(-18) × (+7).

First determine the sign. Because one factor is negative and the other is positive, the product is negative.

Next calculate the magnitude:

18 × 7 = 126.

Combining the sign and magnitude gives:

**-126.**

This two-part procedure makes multiplication more reliable, particularly when factors have large absolute values.

### 6.1 Repeated Financial Transactions

Integer multiplication is useful for calculating repeated financial changes of equal value. A positive amount may represent money received, while a negative amount may represent money spent or lost. Multiplication combines the repeated changes into one numerical expression.

### 6.2 Temperature Changes

A temperature that decreases by an equal number of Celsius degrees during consecutive periods can be modeled through multiplication.

Suppose a hypothetical temperature record shows a decrease of 3°C every hour for five hours.

The total change is:

5 × (-3°C) = -15°C.

If the starting temperature is 12°C, then the final temperature would be:

12°C + (-15°C) = -3°C.

The first calculation determines the total signed change, while the second applies that change to the initial measurement.

### 6.3 Elevation and Repeated Movement

Vertical movement can be represented through signed distances. Upward movement may be defined as positive, while downward movement is negative.

A diver who descends 4 meters per stage for seven stages has a total signed displacement of:

7 × (-4 m) = -28 m.

The product represents a movement of 28 meters downward from the starting position.

The calculation does not automatically identify the diver's final elevation unless the starting elevation is known.

### 6.4 Multiplication with Negative Time Displacements

A negative factor can represent a backward step along a signed time axis when this interpretation is clearly defined.

Suppose a quantity changes at a constant rate of -2 units per hour. A time displacement of -3 hours means moving three hours backward from a reference time.

The corresponding change is:

(-2 units/hour) × (-3 hours) = +6 units.

This interpretation illustrates that multiplying two negative quantities can produce a positive signed change. The model depends on a mathematically defined rate and time displacement rather than a literal negative number of physical groups.

### Background: Extending Division to Signed Numbers

Division is a fundamental mathematical operation used to determine equal groups, the size of each group, rates, and relationships between quantities. In earlier mathematics lessons, learners divided positive whole numbers by nonzero positive whole numbers. For example, distributing twenty-four objects equally among six groups produces four objects per group. This relationship is expressed as 24 ÷ 6 = 4.

The study of integers extends division beyond positive numbers. Signed quantities arise in situations involving gains and losses, financial changes, temperature, elevation, and directional movements. When such quantities must be divided into equal parts, the sign of the dividend or divisor influences the interpretation of the quotient.

Consider a school organization that records an expense of ₱480 across six identical activities. If the total expense is represented as -₱480, the expense per activity can be expressed as (-480) ÷ 6 = -80. The negative quotient indicates an expense of ₱80 for each activity. The division operation determines the magnitude of each equal share, while the negative sign identifies its financial direction.

Integer division is closely connected with multiplication. If 6 × (-8) = -48, then (-48) ÷ 6 = -8. This inverse relationship makes multiplication a reliable method for understanding and verifying division. Instead of treating division rules as unrelated procedures, learners can derive them from multiplication facts established in the preceding lesson.

The division sign rules follow the same sign relationships as multiplication. Dividing two nonzero integers with the same sign produces a positive quotient. Dividing two nonzero integers with different signs produces a negative quotient. The magnitude of the quotient is determined by dividing the absolute values of the dividend and divisor.

However, integer division has important limitations. Unlike integer addition, subtraction, and multiplication, dividing one integer by another does not always produce an integer. For example, 7 ÷ 2 = 3.5, which is not an integer. This distinction introduces the idea that a mathematical operation may be defined without remaining within a particular number set.

Division by zero presents a different issue. An expression such as 12 ÷ 0 does not have a defined numerical value. The inverse relationship with multiplication explains why: there is no number that can be multiplied by zero to produce twelve. Similarly, 0 ÷ 0 cannot be assigned a unique answer because every number multiplied by zero produces zero.

In addition to learning division procedures, learners must apply the order of operations to expressions involving several arithmetic operations. Parentheses, exponents, multiplication, division, addition, and subtraction must be handled according to their established hierarchy. Multiplication and division have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right; addition and subtraction also have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right.

## SECTION 2. DIVISION AS THE INVERSE OF MULTIPLICATION

### 2.1 The Meaning of Integer Division

Division is the inverse operation of multiplication. The inverse relationship provides a way to determine an unknown factor when the product and the other nonzero factor are known.

Consider the multiplication statement:

6 × 4 = 24.

The related division statements are:

24 ÷ 6 = 4

24 ÷ 4 = 6.

These relationships remain valid for signed integers. If:

6 × (-4) = -24,

then:

(-24) ÷ 6 = -4

and:

(-24) ÷ (-4) = 6.

The relationship between multiplication and division is not changed by the presence of negative signs. Instead, the signs determine which multiplication fact is appropriate.

### 2.2 Dividend, Divisor, and Quotient

Every division expression contains a dividend, a divisor, and a quotient.

The **dividend** is the number being divided. The **divisor** is the nonzero number by which the dividend is divided. The **quotient** is the result.

For example:

(-36) ÷ 9 = -4.

Here, -36 is the dividend, 9 is the divisor, and -4 is the quotient.

To verify the result, multiply the quotient by the divisor:

(-4) × 9 = -36.

The product agrees with the original dividend, confirming that the quotient is correct.

> **FORMULA CARD - INVERSE RELATIONSHIP**
>
> **a ÷ b = q if and only if b × q = a**
>
> Where:
>
> - a = dividend
> - b = nonzero divisor
> - q = quotient
>
> **Restriction: b ≠ 0**
>
> The quotient may be an integer or a non-integer rational number, depending on the values of a and b.

### 2.3 Division as Equal Sharing

A concrete model of integer division can use equal groups of signed counters.

Suppose a total change of -18 points is shared equally across three rounds. Represent -18 using eighteen negative counters. Arrange the counters into three equal groups.

Each group contains six negative counters, representing -6.

Therefore:

(-18) ÷ 3 = -6.

This model establishes a useful interpretation: a negative quantity distributed into a positive number of equal groups gives a negative value for each group.

However, a negative divisor cannot literally represent a negative number of physical groups. Such expressions are better understood through the inverse relationship with multiplication and through signed-number patterns.

## SECTION 3. DIVISION SIGN RULES AND ZERO

### 3.1 Sign Relationships in Integer Division

The sign of an integer quotient is determined by the signs of the dividend and divisor, provided that the divisor is nonzero and the dividend is not zero for the usual positive-or-negative classification.

When two nonzero integers have the same sign, their quotient is positive. When they have different signs, their quotient is negative.

The numerical magnitude is calculated separately by dividing the absolute values.

For example:

(+48) ÷ (+6) = +8.

(-48) ÷ (-6) = +8.

(+48) ÷ (-6) = -8.

(-48) ÷ (+6) = -8.

All four quotients have an absolute value of 8. Their signs differ according to the sign combinations.

### 3.2 Deriving the Division Sign Rules

The division sign rules can be derived from multiplication.

Because (+6) × (+7) = +42, it follows that:

(+42) ÷ (+6) = +7.

Because (-6) × (-7) = +42:

(+42) ÷ (-6) = -7.

Because (+6) × (-7) = -42:

(-42) ÷ (+6) = -7.

Because (-6) × (+7) = -42:

(-42) ÷ (-6) = +7.

These examples demonstrate that division is consistent with multiplication, including the signs of its results.

> **FORMULA CARD - INTEGER DIVISION SIGN RULES**
>
> **Positive ÷ Positive = Positive**
>
> **Negative ÷ Negative = Positive**
>
> **Positive ÷ Negative = Negative**
>
> **Negative ÷ Positive = Negative**
>
> **Zero ÷ Nonzero Integer = Zero**
>
> **Division by zero is undefined.**
>
> For a defined division expression, divide the absolute values and apply the sign determined by the dividend and divisor.

### 3.4 Dividing Zero by a Nonzero Integer

Zero divided by any nonzero integer equals zero.

For example:

0 ÷ 5 = 0.

0 ÷ (-8) = 0.

The inverse relationship confirms the results:

5 × 0 = 0.

(-8) × 0 = 0.

The divisor must remain nonzero. The fact that zero can be a dividend does not mean that zero can be a divisor.

### 3.5 Why Division by Zero Is Undefined

Consider 12 ÷ 0.

If its quotient were some number q, then the inverse relationship would require:

0 × q = 12.

However, zero multiplied by any number equals zero. There is no number q satisfying the equation. Consequently, 12 ÷ 0 is undefined.

Now consider 0 ÷ 0.

The inverse relationship would require:

0 × q = 0.

Every number satisfies this equation. There is no unique quotient. Consequently, 0 ÷ 0 is also undefined.

The two cases demonstrate different difficulties: a nonzero dividend divided by zero has no solution under the inverse relationship, while zero divided by zero fails to identify a unique solution.

Neither expression should be assigned a numerical answer.

### 3.6 Division Is Not Always Closed in the Integers

A set is closed under an operation when applying that operation to members of the set always produces another member of the set.

Integers are closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication.

However, integers are not closed under division.

For example:

7 ÷ 2 = 3.5.

The dividend and divisor are integers, but the quotient is not an integer.

Therefore, not every division problem involving integers has an integer result. The quotient may belong to the rational numbers instead.

### 3.7 Division Is Neither Commutative nor Associative

Changing the order of a division expression generally changes the result.

For example:

24 ÷ 6 = 4.

But:

6 ÷ 24 = ¼.

The results are unequal. Division is therefore not commutative.

Regrouping division expressions can also change their values.

Consider:

(48 ÷ 8) ÷ 2 = 6 ÷ 2 = 3.

But:

48 ÷ (8 ÷ 2) = 48 ÷ 4 = 12.

Since 3 ≠ 12, division is not associative.

These properties become especially important when simplifying expressions containing several division operations.

### 4.1 A Reliable Division Procedure

Integer division can be carried out systematically by separating the determination of the sign from the computation of the quotient's magnitude.

The first step is to identify the dividend and divisor. Before dividing, the learner must verify that the divisor is not zero. Next, the learner determines whether the signs of the two nonzero integers are the same or different.

The absolute values are then divided using ordinary division facts. Finally, the proper sign is assigned to the quotient.

The result should be checked by multiplying the quotient by the divisor. This procedure is particularly valuable when the original expression contains negative numbers because it allows learners to identify both magnitude errors and sign errors.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6A - DIVIDING TWO NEGATIVE INTEGERS

**Problem:** Evaluate (-96) ÷ (-12).

**Given:** Dividend = -96; divisor = -12.

**Find:** Quotient.

**Formula:** Dividend ÷ Divisor = Quotient, provided that the divisor is nonzero.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the signs: both the dividend and divisor are negative.
2. Determine the quotient's sign: two negative integers produce a positive quotient.
3. Find the absolute values: |-96| = 96 and |-12| = 12.
4. Divide the magnitudes: 96 ÷ 12 = 8.
5. Apply the positive sign and verify: (-12) × (+8) = -96.

**Final Answer: (-96) ÷ (-12) = +8.**

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6B - DIVIDING INTEGERS WITH DIFFERENT SIGNS

**Problem:** Evaluate (+135) ÷ (-9).

**Given:** Dividend = +135; divisor = -9.

**Find:** Quotient.

**Principle:** Division of two nonzero integers with different signs produces a negative quotient.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify that the dividend is positive and the divisor is negative.
2. Determine that the quotient must be negative.
3. Find the absolute values: 135 and 9.
4. Divide the magnitudes: 135 ÷ 9 = 15.
5. Apply the negative sign and verify: (-9) × (-15) = +135.

**Final Answer: (+135) ÷ (-9) = -15.**

### 4.2 Verifying Division Using Multiplication

Multiplication is an essential tool for checking division. The relationship is:

**Quotient × Divisor = Dividend.**

For example:

(-84) ÷ (+7) = -12.

Verification:

(-12) × (+7) = -84.

The verification establishes that the calculated quotient produces the original dividend when multiplied by the divisor.

A correct magnitude with the wrong sign fails this test. If a learner incorrectly reports +12 for the preceding example, multiplication would give +84 rather than -84.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6C - FINDING AN UNKNOWN DIVIDEND

**Problem:** An integer divided by -8 gives a quotient of +6. Determine the dividend.

**Given:** Divisor = -8; quotient = +6.

**Find:** Dividend.

**Formula:** Dividend = Divisor × Quotient.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Write the unknown division relationship: a ÷ (-8) = +6.
2. Apply the inverse operation: a = (-8) × (+6).
3. Identify the signs as different.
4. Multiply the absolute values: 8 × 6 = 48.
5. Assign the negative sign and check: (-48) ÷ (-8) = +6.

**Final Answer: The dividend is -48.**

### 4.3 Non-Integer Quotients

A division expression containing integers may produce a rational number that is not an integer.

For example:

(-15) ÷ 4 = -15/4 = -3.75.

The quotient is defined because the divisor is nonzero. However, the result does not belong to the set of integers.

The distinction between undefined division and non-integer division must be maintained.

The expression 15 ÷ 4 has a defined quotient of 3.75. The expression 15 ÷ 0 is undefined.

One situation produces a valid non-integer number; the other does not produce a valid numerical quotient.

### 4.4 Dividing More Than Two Integers

When several division operations appear without parentheses, they are performed from left to right.

Consider:

(-120) ÷ (-5) ÷ 4.

First:

(-120) ÷ (-5) = +24.

Then:

24 ÷ 4 = 6.

Therefore:

**(-120) ÷ (-5) ÷ 4 = +6.**

Grouping the last two values instead would represent a different expression:

(-120) ÷ [(-5) ÷ 4] = (-120) ÷ (-1.25) = 96.

The example shows why grouping and evaluation order matter. Parentheses are required when a grouping different from the standard left-to-right sequence is intended.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6D - SEQUENTIAL DIVISION

**Problem:** Evaluate (-288) ÷ (-12) ÷ (-3).

**Given:** Three integers -288, -12, and -3.

**Find:** Value of the expression.

**Principle:** Evaluate division operations from left to right.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the first division: (-288) ÷ (-12).
2. Apply the same-sign rule: the first quotient is positive.
3. Calculate the first quotient: 288 ÷ 12 = 24.
4. Divide by the remaining integer: 24 ÷ (-3) = -8.
5. Verify: the first quotient is 24, and 24 ÷ (-3) = -8.

**Final Answer: -8.**

## SECTION 5. MATHEMATICAL REASONING AND COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

### 5.1 Sign Rules Are Determined by the Factors

The sign of an integer quotient is related to the multiplication fact that verifies it.

Suppose:

(-63) ÷ (-9) = q.

The quotient must satisfy:

(-9) × q = -63.

If q were negative, the multiplication of two negative integers would produce a positive result, contradicting the required negative dividend.

The correct quotient is +7 because:

(-9) × (+7) = -63.

This argument justifies the division sign rule rather than simply applying a memorized statement.

### 5.2 Dividing by One and Negative One

Every integer divided by +1 equals itself.

**a ÷ 1 = a.**

Examples:

-27 ÷ 1 = -27.

35 ÷ 1 = 35.

Dividing an integer by -1 produces its opposite.

**a ÷ (-1) = -a.**

Examples:

-27 ÷ (-1) = +27.

35 ÷ (-1) = -35.

These properties allow quick simplification and provide an additional way to understand signed division.

### 5.3 Dividing Zero

Zero divided by a nonzero integer is zero because multiplying the proposed quotient zero by the divisor returns the original dividend.

For example:

0 ÷ (-6) = 0.

Verification:

(-6) × 0 = 0.

Division by zero is never permitted in ordinary arithmetic, including when the dividend is zero.

A learner must not confuse the statements 0 ÷ 6 = 0 and 6 ÷ 0 is undefined.

### 5.4 Error-Analysis Matrix

| Incorrect Statement | Nature of the Error | Correct Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| (-48) ÷ (-6) = -8 | Same-sign rule applied incorrectly | The quotient is +8 |
| (+56) ÷ (-7) = +8 | Different-sign rule ignored | The quotient is -8 |
| 0 ÷ (-4) is undefined | Confuses a zero dividend with a zero divisor | The quotient is 0 |
| (-12) ÷ 0 = 0 | Attempts division by zero | The expression is undefined |
| 7 ÷ 2 is undefined | Confuses a non-integer quotient with undefined division | The quotient is 3.5 |
| 36 ÷ 6 ÷ 2 = 12 | Performs the second division first without parentheses | The result is 3 |
| 18 ÷ (-3) × 2 = -3 | Does not follow left-to-right order | The result is -12 |

### 5.5 Watch Out: Division Does Not Always Preserve Integers

Division differs from addition, subtraction, and multiplication because the quotient of two integers is not necessarily another integer.

For example:

9 ÷ 4 = 2.25.

The result is a rational number but not an integer.

However:

12 ÷ 4 = 3.

This quotient is an integer.

Determining whether an integer division is exact requires examining whether the divisor divides the dividend without a fractional remainder.

### 5.6 Watch Out: The Sign of a Quotient Is Not Its Magnitude

Consider:

(-72) ÷ (+8) = -9.

The absolute value of the quotient is 9, while the quotient itself is -9.

The magnitude answers how far the result lies from zero. The sign determines which side of zero contains the result.

Both features are required for a complete and correct answer.

### 5.7 Error Investigation

A learner writes:

(-84) ÷ (-7) = -12.

The learner divides 84 by 7 correctly but assigns the wrong sign.

To identify the mistake, multiply the proposed quotient by the divisor:

(-12) × (-7) = +84.

The result does not equal the original dividend -84.

Now use the correct quotient:

(+12) × (-7) = -84.

The original dividend is recovered.

Therefore, the correct quotient is **+12**.

<WritingBlock id="73618" variant="document">## SECTION 6. APPLIED MASTERY - GEMDAS AND INTEGRATED INTEGER OPERATIONS

### 6.1 Why the Order of Operations Matters

Mathematical expressions frequently contain more than one operation. Without a consistent order of evaluation, the same expression could produce different answers.

Consider:

18 - 12 ÷ 3.

Performing subtraction first gives 6 ÷ 3 = 2, but this is not the correct interpretation under the standard order of operations.

Division must be performed before subtraction:

18 - 12 ÷ 3

= 18 - 4

= 14.

The standard order ensures that mathematical expressions have a consistent meaning.

### 6.2 GEMDAS

The acronym **GEMDAS** provides a guide for simplifying numerical expressions.

| Letter | Mathematical Operation | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| G | Grouping symbols | Evaluate expressions within grouping symbols first |
| E | Exponents | Evaluate powers after grouping |
| M and D | Multiplication and Division | Equal priority; evaluate from left to right |
| A and S | Addition and Subtraction | Equal priority; evaluate from left to right |

Multiplication does not automatically come before division. Addition does not automatically come before subtraction. Operations with equal priority are evaluated in the order in which they appear from left to right.

Grouping symbols include parentheses, brackets, and braces. An expression may contain nested grouping symbols, in which case the innermost grouped expression is evaluated first.

> **FORMULA CARD - GEMDAS EVALUATION RULE**
>
> **Grouping → Exponents → Multiplication and Division → Addition and Subtraction**
>
> **Equal-priority operations are evaluated from left to right.**
>
> Exponents indicate repeated multiplication of a base. For example, 3² = 3 × 3 = 9.
>
> No division step may use zero as its divisor.

### 6.3 Exponents and Negative Integers

Exponents require special attention when the base is negative.

Consider:

(-3)² = (-3) × (-3) = +9.

The parentheses show that the negative sign is part of the base being squared.

In contrast:

-3² = -(3 × 3) = -9.

Without parentheses, the exponent applies to 3 before the external negative sign is applied.

The expressions therefore have different values.

When evaluating powers of negative integers, parentheses determine whether the sign belongs to the base.

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6E - DIVISION AND SUBTRACTION

**Problem:** Simplify 24 - (-36) ÷ 6.

**Given:** Numerical expression 24 - (-36) ÷ 6.

**Find:** Value of the expression.

**Principle:** Perform division before subtraction.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Identify the division operation: (-36) ÷ 6.
2. Apply the different-sign rule: (-36) ÷ 6 = -6.
3. Substitute the quotient: 24 - (-6).
4. Rewrite subtraction as addition of the opposite: 24 + 6.
5. Add: 24 + 6 = 30.

**Final Answer: 30.**

### WORKED EXAMPLE 3.6F - GROUPING, EXPONENTS, AND DIVISION

**Problem:** Simplify 5 + [(-3)² × 4 - 24] ÷ (-6).

**Given:** Numerical expression 5 + [(-3)² × 4 - 24] ÷ (-6).

**Find:** Value of the expression.

**Principle:** Apply GEMDAS, evaluating grouped operations in the correct order.

**Step-by-Step Solution**

1. Evaluate the exponent: (-3)² = 9.
2. Multiply within the brackets: 9 × 4 = 36.
3. Complete the grouped subtraction: 36 - 24 = 12.
4. Divide by -6: 12 ÷ (-6) = -2.
5. Add the remaining terms: 5 + (-2) = 3.

**Final Answer: 3.**

**Verification:** The complete grouped expression has value 12. The quotient 12 ÷ (-6) is -2, and adding it to 5 gives 3.

### 6.4 Why Multiplication and Division Have Equal Priority

Consider:

48 ÷ 6 × 2.

Correctly evaluating from left to right gives:

48 ÷ 6 × 2

= 8 × 2

= 16.

Multiplying 6 × 2 first would give 48 ÷ 12 = 4, which represents a different grouping.

To obtain 4, the expression must explicitly include parentheses:

48 ÷ (6 × 2) = 4.

This distinction explains why the relative positions of multiplication and division must be respected.

### 6.5 Why Addition and Subtraction Have Equal Priority

Consider:

20 - 8 + 3.

Evaluating from left to right gives:

20 - 8 + 3

= 12 + 3

= 15.

An incorrect approach that adds 8 and 3 first would calculate 20 - 11 = 9. That operation changes the intended grouping.

The expression 20 - (8 + 3) does equal 9, but the parentheses are essential.

### 6.6 Absolute Value in Integrated Expressions

Absolute value symbols can function as grouping instructions when evaluating numerical expressions.

For example:

|-12| ÷ (-3) + 5

= 12 ÷ (-3) + 5

= -4 + 5

= 1.

The absolute value is evaluated first. Division follows, and addition is performed last.

Absolute value does not change the established priority of other operations; it establishes the value of the enclosed expression before that value is used.

### 6.7 Contextual Application: Equal Financial Changes

Suppose a youth organization records a total expense of ₱1,260 distributed equally across seven community activities.

The expense per activity is:

(-1,260) ÷ 7 = -180.

If the organization later receives ₱250 for each of the seven activities, the net change per activity is:

-180 + 250 = +70.

The net change across all seven activities is:

7 × (+70) = +490.

The positive total indicates that the combined receipts exceed the recorded expenses by ₱490.

The calculation integrates division, addition, multiplication, and consistent financial sign conventions.

### LESSON SUMMARY

> **DIVIDING INTEGERS - CONCEPTUAL SYNTHESIS**
>
> Integer division is the inverse operation of multiplication. If a ÷ b = q, where b is nonzero, then b × q = a. This relationship provides both a conceptual explanation and a verification method for division.
>
> The dividend is the quantity being divided, the divisor is the nonzero number by which it is divided, and the quotient is the result. When the dividend and divisor are nonzero integers with the same sign, their quotient is positive. When they have different signs, the quotient is negative.
>
> The magnitude of a quotient is determined by dividing the absolute values of the dividend and divisor. The correct sign is then assigned according to the division sign rules.
>
> Zero divided by any nonzero integer equals zero. Division by zero is undefined because the inverse relationship with multiplication cannot produce an appropriate unique quotient.
>
> Division is neither commutative nor associative. Changing the order or grouping of division operations can alter the result. Furthermore, integers are not closed under division because dividing two integers may produce a non-integer rational number.
>
> The GEMDAS order of operations establishes a consistent procedure for simplifying numerical expressions. Grouping symbols are evaluated first, followed by exponents. Multiplication and division have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right. Addition and subtraction also have equal priority and are evaluated from left to right.
>
> Correct integer division and multistep calculations require attention to numerical signs, grouping symbols, units, and mathematical verification. These concepts support further study of algebra and the interpretation of real-world quantitative situations.

# PAGE 51 - QUARTER 3 SYNTHESIS AND MATHEMATICAL GLOSSARY

## INTEGRATED QUARTERLY SYNTHESIS

The third quarter of Grade 7 Mathematics develops two essential areas of mathematical proficiency: interpreting statistical information and reasoning with integers. Although these areas involve different procedures, both require careful attention to the meaning of numbers, the structure of mathematical representations, and the reliability of conclusions.

### Statistics as a Foundation for Evidence-Based Decisions

Statistical investigations provide methods for collecting, organizing, presenting, and interpreting information. A well-designed investigation begins with a specific question and a clearly identified population. The investigator determines which variable will be observed, how information will be obtained, and whether the investigation requires a census or a sample.

A population contains all the individuals or objects relevant to the investigation. A sample contains only selected members. When samples are used, the method of selection influences whether the results reasonably represent the population. Simple random and appropriately designed stratified sampling provide ways to reduce selection bias. Convenience sampling may be easier to conduct but presents greater limitations when conclusions are extended to a larger population.

Observations may be categorical, discrete numerical, or continuous numerical. Categorical data identify classifications; discrete data are obtained by counting; and continuous data are obtained by measurement. Recognizing the data type helps determine which statistical methods and representations are appropriate.

A frequency distribution table organizes observations by reporting how often each category or value occurs. Its accuracy can be checked by confirming that category frequencies add to the total number of observations. Relative frequency and percentage frequency allow comparisons of categories in relation to the complete data set.

Statistical graphs present information visually. Bar graphs compare distinct categories or values. Pie graphs illustrate parts of a whole using sector angles that total 360°. Line graphs reveal changes over ordered observations, especially across time. Stem-and-leaf plots preserve the original numerical values while organizing them by place value.

A correctly constructed graph must use accurate labels, scales, and units. Mathematical interpretation should be based on the values actually represented rather than on visual impressions alone. A graph may demonstrate a relationship or change without establishing its cause. Statistical reasoning therefore requires an awareness of both the evidence and its limitations.

### Integers as Signed Numerical Quantities

The set of integers extends the whole numbers by including negative values. Integers consist of the positive counting numbers, their negative counterparts, and zero. They are represented by the mathematical symbol ℤ.

Negative and positive integers are useful because many real-world quantities have opposite directions or conditions. Examples include financial gains and losses, elevation above and below a reference level, increases and decreases in temperature, and movements in opposite directions.

A number line provides a geometric representation of integer values. Greater integers are located farther to the right, while smaller integers are located farther to the left. Every positive integer is greater than zero, and every negative integer is less than zero. Among negative integers, the integer closer to zero is greater.

Opposite integers have equal distances from zero but different signs, except that zero is its own opposite. Absolute value represents this nonnegative distance. For example, |-12| = 12 and |12| = 12. However, the original signed integers remain different.

### The Four Fundamental Operations

Integer addition combines signed quantities. When the addends have the same sign, their absolute values are added and the common sign is retained. When they have different signs, the smaller absolute value is subtracted from the larger, and the nonzero result takes the sign of the addend having the greater absolute value.

Counters and zero pairs provide concrete explanations for these procedures. Each positive counter represents +1, each negative counter represents -1, and a positive-negative pair represents zero. Number lines provide corresponding geometric explanations through rightward and leftward movement.

Integer subtraction is equivalent to adding the opposite. For any integers a and b, the relationship is a - b = a + (-b). This principle can be demonstrated through counters and verified through the inverse relationship with addition. Subtraction is not commutative or associative in general, so the order and grouping of values must be preserved.

Integer multiplication extends the concept of repeated addition and follows consistent sign rules. Two nonzero factors with the same sign produce a positive product; factors with different signs produce a negative product. Multiplication by zero produces zero. The positive product of two negative integers can be justified using numerical patterns and the distributive property.

When multiplying several nonzero integers, an even number of negative factors produces a positive product, while an odd number produces a negative product. The commutative, associative, distributive, identity, zero, and closure properties support efficient multiplication procedures.

Integer division is the inverse of multiplication. Dividing nonzero integers with the same sign produces a positive quotient, while different signs produce a negative quotient. The quotient is verified by multiplying it by the divisor. Unlike the other three fundamental operations, division is not closed in the integers. A quotient such as 7 ÷ 2 is defined but is not an integer. Division by zero is undefined.

### Integrated Mathematical Reasoning

Multistep expressions must be simplified using a consistent order of operations. GEMDAS places grouping symbols first, followed by exponents. Multiplication and division share equal priority and are performed from left to right. Addition and subtraction likewise share equal priority and are performed from left to right.

The quarter's two mathematical domains complement one another in practical investigations. A school project may use statistics to measure learner preferences and integer operations to calculate revenues, expenses, and projected balances. Statistical results help identify priorities, while signed arithmetic determines whether a proposed activity is financially feasible.

Mathematical proficiency requires more than correct answers. It involves selecting appropriate representations, understanding why procedures work, verifying results independently, using consistent measurement units, identifying possible errors, and communicating conclusions responsibly.

The central lesson of Quarter 3 is that **numbers become useful evidence when they are collected carefully, represented accurately, operated on correctly, and interpreted within their proper context.**
