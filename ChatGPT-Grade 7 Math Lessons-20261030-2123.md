### Background: From Observations to Evidence

Statistics is the study of collecting, organizing, analyzing, and interpreting data. Data are recorded observations, measurements, or responses. A statistical question anticipates variability: its answers may differ among people, objects, or occasions. “How many minutes do Grade 7 learners spend traveling to school?” is statistical because travel times vary. “How many minutes are in one hour?” has a fixed answer and does not require a statistical investigation.

A useful investigation begins with a clear question and a defined group. It then uses a suitable collection method, organizes the observations, and chooses representations that make important patterns visible. Interpretation comes after these steps. A graph cannot repair an unclear question, missing observations, or a sample that systematically excludes part of the population.

In this lesson, all supplied numerical datasets are constructed practice examples, not reported findings about Lake View Adventist Academy. You will use them to examine collection decisions, build frequency tables, and select displays. Your explanations should state what the evidence shows and identify limits on what can reasonably be concluded.

## 2. Subtopic 1: Data Collection and Sampling

The **population** is the complete group of interest; a **sample** is a selected subset. A census studies every member. The **variable** is the characteristic recorded. Categorical data describe labels, such as transport method. Numerical data express counts or measurements. Counts are discrete; measurements such as travel time are continuous, even when rounded to minutes. Student identification numbers are categorical identifiers: adding them has no useful quantitative meaning.

Questionnaires record responses; interviews allow clarification; observation records events; measurement uses instruments and units. Existing records can supply suitable information. Ask neutral questions with a clear time period and nonoverlapping categories. “How did you travel the greatest distance to school today?” accommodates journeys involving several transport methods. Obtain teacher approval and avoid unnecessary names or sensitive information.

## 4. Formula Card and Worked Examples

> ### Formula Card: Relative Frequency and Pie-Graph Angles
>
> **Relative frequency = f/n**
>
> **Percentage = (f/n) × 100%**
>
> **Sector angle = (f/n) × 360°**
>
> Here, f is a category’s frequency and n is the total number of valid observations, with n > 0. Frequencies are counts; sector angles are measured in degrees. Categories for one pie graph must partition the same whole.

### Worked Example 1: Finding a Percentage

**Problem:** In the twelve-learner dataset, what percentage carried no reusable container?

1. **Given:** Frequency f = 3 learners; total n = 12 learners.
2. **Find:** Percentage of observed learners carrying zero containers.
3. **Formula:** Percentage = (f/n) × 100%.
4. **Step Solution:** 3/12 = 1/4 - simplify the fraction. Then (1/4) × 100% = 25% - convert to a percentage.
5. **Final Answer:** **25% of the twelve observed learners carried no reusable container.** Check: one quarter of 12 is 3.

### Worked Example 2: Constructing a Pie-Graph Sector

**Problem:** Of 20 surveyed learners, 8 walk, 6 use tricycles, 4 use jeepneys, and 2 use bicycles. Find the walking sector’s angle.

1. **Given:** Walking frequency f = 8; total n = 20.
2. **Find:** Central angle representing walking.
3. **Formula:** Sector angle = (f/n) × 360°.
4. **Step Solution:** 8/20 = 0.4 - express the share. Then 0.4 × 360° = 144° - multiply by one full turn.
5. **Final Answer:** **The walking sector measures 144°.** The remaining angles are 108°, 72°, and 36°; all four total 360°.

## 5. Mathematical Reasoning and Common Misconceptions

Doubling all counts changes 8 walkers among 20 learners to 16 among 40. Since 8/20 = 16/40, percentages and sector angles remain unchanged. Why must a pie graph state its total?

> **Watch Out:** A largest category need not be a majority: 40% is less than half. Sample results do not automatically describe populations, and graphs alone do not prove causes.

## 6. Subtopic 3: Choosing and Interpreting Graphs

A **bar graph** compares categories using equal-width, separated bars. Label axes, use equal numerical intervals, and normally start the frequency axis at zero. A **pie graph** represents parts of one whole: category angles must total 360°. A **line graph** shows change across an ordered variable, commonly time. Plot paired values and join consecutive points. Joining unrelated categories misleadingly suggests continuity.

A **stem-and-leaf plot** preserves numerical observations by place value. For times 12, 15, 15, 18, 21, 23, 24, 27 minutes, stem 1 has leaves 2, 5, 5, 8; stem 2 has leaves 1, 3, 4, 7. The key states that stem 1 with leaf 2 means 12 minutes. Arrange stems and leaves in increasing order. Repeated leaves represent separate observations and must remain. Four times are in the teens and four in the twenties.

### Background: Extending Numbers beyond Zero

Whole numbers describe many everyday counts: zero absent learners, three notebooks, or twenty collected containers. However, a count alone cannot distinguish opposite relationships. A location five metres above a reference level differs from one five metres below it. Both involve five metres, but the directions are opposite. Signed numbers record this difference without requiring a separate verbal explanation every time.

Integers include the whole numbers and their negative counterparts. They allow us to describe positions, changes, and comparisons relative to zero. Zero must have a defined meaning: it may represent a starting location, a target amount, or a chosen measurement level. A negative sign does not automatically mean that something is wrong, undesirable, or physically impossible. It describes a mathematical relationship within an agreed system.

This lesson develops three connected ideas: sign indicates direction or position relative to a reference, order indicates which value is greater, and absolute value measures distance from zero. Keeping these ideas distinct prepares you to explain integer operations rather than memorize sign rules without understanding them.

## 2. Subtopic 1: Integers, Opposites, and Reference Points

The **integers**, denoted by ℤ, include zero, positive counting numbers, and their negatives. Examples are -12, -3, 0, 4, and 19. Integers extend indefinitely. Values such as ½ and 2.7 are not integers. Classification depends on value, not appearance: 6/3 equals 2, and 4.0 equals 4, so both represent integers. Zero is an integer but is neither positive nor negative.

Positive integers exceed zero; negative integers are less than zero. The positive sign is optional: +7 = 7. Addition combines quantities into a sum. With whole numbers, adding usually suggests an increase because the quantities being added are nonnegative. ## 6. Subtopic 3: Properties and Efficient Calculation

### Background: Subtraction beyond Taking Away

Subtraction can describe removing a quantity, comparing two quantities, or finding a missing change. These meanings are related, but the context determines how the subtraction expression must be written. If a temperature changes from -4 °C to 3 °C, the change is final temperature minus initial temperature. The calculation 3 - (-4) gives an increase of 7 °C. Simply subtracting the unsigned digits would lose the relationship between the readings.

With whole numbers, subtraction is often introduced by removing objects from a collection. Integer subtraction extends this model using positive and negative counters. Sometimes the required counters are not initially present. Adding zero pairs provides them while preserving the collection’s value. Removing the specified counters then reveals the difference.

This lesson connects that concrete process to number-line movements and the rule for adding the opposite. It also distinguishes signed differences from nonnegative distances. Every answer should be checked against the question: are you reporting a remaining quantity, an increase or decrease, or the separation between two positions? ## 2. Subtopic 1: Subtraction as Adding the Opposite

In a - b, a is the **minuend**, b the **subtrahend**, and the result the **difference**. Include the subtrahend’s sign: in 5 - (-3), it is -3. Subtraction reverses addition: if a - b = c, then c + b = a. Thus, 5 - (-3) = 8 because 8 + (-3) = 5. Adding the subtrahend back checks the difference.

Calculate a - b by adding b’s opposite: a - b = a + (-b). Adding b back gives [a + (-b)] + b = a + [(-b) + b] = a, since opposites sum to zero. Therefore, 7 - 4 = 7 + (-4), while 7 - (-4) = 7 + 4. Keep the first number unchanged; replace subtraction with addition and the second number with its opposite. Do not change every sign.

## 6. Subtopic 3: Changes, Comparisons, and Verification

A signed change compares a final value with an initial value in that order. From -8 m to -3 m, the change is -3 - (-8) = +5 m. Reversing the journey gives -8 - (-3) = -5 m. The magnitudes agree, but their signs indicate opposite directions. For a comparison, name the order: “A minus B” is different from “B minus A.” If A has a signed score of -2 and B has -9, then A - B = 7, so A’s score is seven points higher. Adding their distances from zero would travel to zero and back unnecessarily. Use the difference, then take its absolute value.

## 3. Subtopic 2: Justifying the Sign Rules

Hold the second factor at -3 and decrease the first factor by one. The products 3 × (-3), 2 × (-3), 1 × (-3), and 0 × (-3) are -9, -6, -3, and 0. Each product increases by 3 because one negative-three contribution is removed. Continuing consistently gives (-1) × (-3) = 3 and (-2) × (-3) = 6. This pattern suggests the rule; the distributive property explains why the pattern must continue. It is not a rule chosen merely to make calculations convenient.

Since 3 + (-3) = 0, we have (-2) × [3 + (-3)] = 0. Distributing gives [(-2) × 3] + [(-2) × (-3)] = 0. The first product is -6, so the second must be +6 to make the sum zero. Therefore, (-2) × (-3) = 6. More generally, multiplication by a negative reverses the sign of a nonzero quantity while scaling its magnitude. For two nonzero factors, the same signs give a positive product and different signs give a negative product. If a factor is zero, use the zero rule instead.

## 5. Mathematical Reasoning and Common Misconceptions

A product of several nonzero integers is positive when it contains an even number of negative factors and negative when it contains an odd number. Pair negative factors: each pair contributes a positive product. An unpaired negative reverses the final sign. > **Watch Out:** Multiplying two negatives gives a positive product, but adding them gives a negative sum. Multiplication does not always increase value: 7 × (-2) = -14, and 7 × 0 = 0. Count negative factors, not negative symbols appearing elsewhere in the problem.

## 6. Subtopic 3: Properties and Applied Calculation

Integer multiplication is **closed**: integer factors produce an integer. It is **commutative**, a × b = b × a, and **associative**, (a × b) × c = a × (b × c). These properties permit convenient rearrangement and grouping. For (-5) × 7 × (-2), group the negative factors: [(-5) × (-2)] × 7 = 10 × 7 = 70. Preserve each factor’s sign. The identity property gives a × 1 = a, while multiplication by -1 produces the opposite. These properties are mathematical relationships, not separate unexplained tricks.

The **distributive property** connects multiplication and addition: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c. For (-6) × 19, write 19 = 20 + (-1). Then (-6) × 19 = (-6) × 20 + (-6) × (-1) = -120 + 6 = -114. Multiply both terms inside the parentheses; omitting one product changes the value. In applications, first confirm that the repeated amount is constant. If daily changes differ, add the individual changes instead of multiplying one day’s amount by the number of days.

### Background: Finding an Unknown Factor

Division answers questions about equal shares, equal groups, and unknown factors. If a practice fund records a total change of -₱120 across four equal payments, the change per payment is -120 ÷ 4 = -30 pesos. Multiplication verifies the result: four changes of -₱30 combine to -₱120. The negative quotient describes the direction of each change, not a negative number of physical payments.

A division expression need not describe sharing objects. The calculation 24 ÷ (-6) asks which number, multiplied by -6, produces 24. The answer is -4 because (-6) × (-4) = 24. Thinking in terms of an unknown factor makes division meaningful even when a literal sharing story is unsuitable.

This final lesson brings together integer operations and the order of operations. You will distinguish exact integer quotients from noninteger results, explain why zero cannot be a divisor, and evaluate expressions containing grouping symbols, powers, products, quotients, sums, and differences. The goal is a justified sequence of equivalent expressions, supported by multiplication checks and accurate interpretation of units.

## 2. Subtopic 1: Division as the Inverse of Multiplication

In a ÷ b = q, a is the **dividend**, b the **divisor**, and q the **quotient**. For b ≠ 0, this means b × q = a. Thus, -18 ÷ 3 = -6 because 3 × (-6) = -18. Distributing eighteen negative counters into three equal groups gives -6 per group. Alternatively, -18 ÷ (-6) = 3 asks how many groups of negative six combine to negative eighteen. Identify whether the unknown is the value per group or the number of groups.

For nonzero dividend and divisor, equal signs give a positive quotient; different signs give a negative quotient. These rules follow from multiplication. In -24 ÷ (-6), the missing factor is +4 because (-6) × 4 = -24. In 24 ÷ (-6), it is -4 because (-6) × (-4) = 24. Divide absolute values for the magnitude, determine the sign, and multiply the quotient by the divisor to check. Recover the signed dividend, not merely its absolute value.

## 3. Subtopic 2: Zero, One, and Division Restrictions

Zero divided by nonzero b is zero because b × 0 = 0. Division by zero is different. If 8 ÷ 0 had quotient q, then 0 × q would have to equal 8. No number satisfies this equation. Therefore, 8 ÷ 0 is undefined. Similarly, 0 ÷ 0 is undefined: every number satisfies 0 × q = 0, so there is no unique quotient. Neither case gives an ordinary numerical answer of zero or infinity.

Dividing by 1 preserves a number: a ÷ 1 = a. Dividing by -1 gives its opposite: a ÷ (-1) = -a. The statement a ÷ a = 1 requires a ≠ 0. Check divisors after simplifying them: 12 ÷ (5 - 5) is undefined because the divisor becomes zero. A zero factor elsewhere does not repair undefined division; 0 × (8 ÷ 0) remains undefined. Restrictions are part of a mathematical statement, not optional notes.

## 5. Mathematical Reasoning and Common Misconceptions

Division is not commutative: 12 ÷ 3 = 4, but 3 ÷ 12 = ¼. It is not associative: (24 ÷ 6) ÷ 2 = 2, while 24 ÷ (6 ÷ 2) = 8. These examples explain why you cannot freely exchange the dividend and divisor or move parentheses. For division, a changed order can change the value or whether the expression is defined.

> **Watch Out:** GEMDAS does not mean “always multiply before dividing” or “always add before subtracting.” In 24 ÷ 6 × 2, work left to right: 4 × 2 = 8. In 10 - 6 + 2, compute 4 + 2 = 6. Preserve all signs and grouping symbols.

## 6. Subtopic 3: Simplifying Mixed Integer Expressions

Begin by reading the whole expression. Identify nested groups, powers, and potential zero divisors. Simplify one stage at a time, copying unchanged parts accurately. In -4 + 3 × (2 - 7), first obtain -4 + 3 × (-5), then -4 + (-15), and finally -19. Starting with -4 + 3 would combine terms before completing their multiplication structure. Each equality must connect expressions with the same value; use a new line when it helps show the transformation clearly.

Number properties can provide an alternative check when used correctly. For -4 + 3 × (2 - 7), distribution gives -4 + 6 + (-21) = -19. This agrees with grouping first. However, division does not distribute across a sum in the divisor: 12 ÷ (2 + 4) = 2, while 12 ÷ 2 + 12 ÷ 4 = 9. Keep the entire divisor together. When writing a fraction using a slash, include parentheses around a grouped numerator or denominator, such as (8 - 12)/(-2), to prevent ambiguous reading.

# MIND MAP, UNIT SYNTHESIS, AND GLOSSARY

## Unit Synthesis

Statistics and integers provide complementary tools for understanding evidence and change. A statistical investigation begins with a question that anticipates variability. Define the population, identify the variable, and choose a collection method suited to the question. A sample should provide a defensible basis for describing the intended group. Random selection can reduce selection bias, but careful wording, participation, and complete recording still matter. A larger convenience sample does not automatically become representative simply because it includes more people.

Organizing data creates opportunities to detect errors and interpret patterns. A frequency table accounts for observations without changing them. Its total should match the number of valid responses. Relative frequency connects a category count with the relevant whole. Bar graphs compare categories; pie graphs show parts of a whole; line graphs reveal changes across an ordered variable; stem-and-leaf plots preserve individual numerical observations. Titles, units, keys, and consistent scales make these displays meaningful. A graph can describe an association or a change without proving its cause.

Integers describe quantities relative to a reference. Negative values, zero, and positive values occupy ordered positions on a number line. Greater values lie farther right, while absolute value measures distance from zero. These are different comparisons: −9 is less than −4, yet its absolute value is greater. Opposites have equal magnitudes and reversed directions. Zero is its own opposite and has absolute value zero. Signs describe mathematical relationships rather than whether a result is desirable, successful, or morally good.

Integer operations follow connected principles. Addition combines signed contributions, with opposite counters forming zero pairs. Subtraction adds the opposite and can describe a remaining quantity, a comparison, or a signed change. Multiplication represents repeated equal contributions when the group count is nonnegative; patterns and distribution justify its extension to negative factors. Division finds an unknown factor and is verified through multiplication. Division by zero is undefined, and dividing integers does not always produce another integer. Each rule must be matched to the operation being performed.

Complex expressions require attention to structure. Evaluate groups and powers before multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. Multiplication and division share priority, as do addition and subtraction; work left to right within each pair. Properties provide alternative calculations only when their conditions are respected. Subtraction and division cannot be freely reordered or regrouped. Parentheses identify a power’s base and keep grouped divisors intact. A correct numerical answer should be supported by equivalent intermediate expressions and appropriate units.

Mathematical competence includes responsible interpretation. A final balance differs from a net change; a signed change differs from a distance; an average rate does not establish a constant rate. Honest reporting preserves these distinctions. When learners acknowledge uncertainty, retain records, correct errors, and explain limitations, mathematics supports trustworthy decisions. In a school service project, these habits connect academic excellence with Christian stewardship: evidence is used carefully so that shared resources and community needs receive thoughtful attention.
